Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP) proposes that only the currently generated state of the universe exists. Earlier states genuinely existed when they were present but no longer exist. Generation is gapless: reality never lapses, and each present state arises from immediately preceding states through lawful temporal and generative progression. What ceases to exist is each earlier state itself, not necessarily the physical systems that were in that state; whether those systems persist depends on their own conditions of persistence. Gapless generation does not require every physical quantity to change smoothly; abrupt changes, including quantum jumps in models that contain them, are compatible with it. The physical consequences of what previously occurred are instantiated in presently existing configurations of matter, fields, and radiation, some of which constitute present records of past events. These consequences and records are not the past itself; they are parts of the currently existing physical reality. The past consists of what actually happened during formerly present stages, while the future has not yet been generated. The paper develops this as a presentist interpretation of relativistic spacetime that leaves Einstein’s equations unchanged. In the emergent geometric regime, the present is identified with the level sets of the cosmological time function: the supremum of proper time along past-directed causal curves, which in a big-bang universe is the maximal proper time elapsed since the initial singularity. By a theorem of Andersson, Galloway, and Howard, these level sets are future Cauchy surfaces whenever cosmological time is regular: every worldline that continues past a level set crosses it exactly once, so the present remains globally well defined even inside collapsed structure, where cosmological time can lose differentiability and its level sets can acquire corners. The view therefore applies insofar as the actual universe is globally hyperbolic with regular cosmological time. The present is a whole spatial slice of the universe, not a point, and what exists is the physical configuration of fields and matter on that slice. Special relativity alone supplies no such slice. It comes from the actual matter-filled cosmological solution of general relativity, fixed by its geometry rather than by any idealized observers. Nothing in cosmological time marks one slice as “now”: the geometry fixes which events are co-present, and the ontology supplies presentness. Identifying these slices with the present is the paper’s interpretive posit, not a theorem, and the present can be located from its past alone, without reference to the future. The generation parameter is a monotone relabeling of this function. It introduces no new field, no new degree of freedom, and no local preferred-frame effect, and it can optionally be expressed through a constrained action that leaves the Einstein equations unchanged. CTGP is dynamic in one precise sense: the present is continuously generated rather than being one slice of a completed block. It is not dynamic in the sense of an observer-dependent or matter-dependent “now.” The paper tests whether generation can be tied to local matter density and argues that, in the classes of laws analyzed, it cannot. An exact density-weighted law drives the generation flow out of alignment with matter within a light-crossing time of any structure, and stable soft completions force a uniform rate inside bound structures, suppressing any density-weighted cosmological effect below one part in 10⁴⁰. Within these classes, the generation rate may vary with cosmic epoch but not across space, and no intrinsic cosmological axis follows. On empirical standing, presentist and eternalist readings of identical physics are shown to be observationally equivalent, since every observation is an observation of present records, so the ontology cannot be verified by measurement at fixed physics. It remains exposed through a structural commitment: the present state and the fundamental laws must suffice to generate its successor, deterministically or stochastically, without indispensable future boundary data or global consistency requirements that preclude an adequate forward-generative account. Quantum randomness does not threaten this, since it bears on which successor occurs, not on whether the present suffices to produce one. Neither does history-dependent behavior such as magnetic hysteresis, whose history is carried by present physical variables. A confirmed chronology violation, interventionist retrocausal dependence, or an indispensable future boundary condition or global consistency requirement that precludes an adequate forward-generative account would refute the view. A unique final state that simply results from deterministic dynamics does not count; only a final condition that must be imposed as an independent input to fix earlier states would. Current physics is consistent with the commitment but does not establish it. Also treated: the truthmaking of past-tense statements, constraints on quantum interpretation (CTGP requires a single realized outcome, which excludes standard Everettian ontology), cosmological observables as causal records, objections from point presentism and special relativity, the relationship to causal set theory and growing-block models, formal results, and an optional extension to conscious experience that the core framework does not presuppose. Quantum reference frames (QRFs) permit different quantum systems to serve as reference frames, raising a foundational question for single-outcome theories: how can frame-dependent descriptions represent one objectively realized history? Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP) holds that only the present exists, that physical reality is generated through successive stages, and that truths about the determinate past are grounded in primitive past-tensed properties of the present world. We develop a mathematical compatibility framework connecting these commitments to QRF covariance, stochastic quantum instruments, and relativistic measurement theory. We first construct an explicit QRF-covariant, single-outcome quantum model using relational GRW-type events and a Lucretian truth profile. We establish a frame-independent determinacy criterion, characterize when different frame descriptions identify the same complete physical state and realized history, and prove covariance of the generative law and its history probabilities. We distinguish physical-state identity, historical identity, and observational indistinguishability, and show that the present quantum state need not determine the complete past. The construction demonstrates the joint mathematical consistency of these commitments without deriving their ontological interpretation or establishing physical adequacy. We then formulate a compatibility program for relativistic quantum field theory, quantum clocks, and quantum gravity. For specified causally local instruments, we establish conditions for foliation-independent outcome histories and operational no-signaling. Free-field detector and Gaussian-collapse constructions identify both sufficient conditions for causal admissibility and obstructions to extending them to arbitrary local operations and to covariantly located collapses. We calculate the energy–momentum cost of Gaussian collapse and examine, without adopting it, a cosmological-time-guided candidate that defines normalized finite-volume dynamics but introduces preferred-frame structure. To distinguish formal collapse prescriptions from physical implementations, we compare centre-time ordering, completion-time ordering, and a local field–pointer coupling. The pointer construction yields foliation-independent statistics, reproduces ordered-collapse predictions for causally orderable configurations with suitably prepared pointers, and defines a consistent law of outcome records even in spatially infinite universes. Interfering collapse regions, however, produce different joint outcome laws, showing that this local coupling does not in general reproduce a prescribed collapse ordering. Definite pointer readings require an explicit single-outcome postulate, and infinite-volume generation of the physical state remains open. We also give clock-neutral descriptions of late-record instruments for two ideal clocks and exhibit interference between alternative clock-dependent interaction orders. These results establish a concrete mathematical compatibility framework, not a completed relativistic theory or an empirical confirmation of CTGP. They separate frame-dependent descriptions from the identity of realized events, identify which features of single-outcome dynamics survive changes of reference frame and foliation, and isolate the additional physical requirements for relativistic collapse, temporal quantum reference frames, and quantum gravity. Reality and information about reality are different categories A record of something is not the thing itself. A photograph of yesterday is not yesterday, a memory of an event is not the event, and a spacetime diagram containing an event is not the event occurring. CTGP builds this distinction into its formalism: its generated domain M(σ) represents the present world’s history mathematically, without implying that earlier stages continue to exist alongside the present. The signal-based epistemology makes the parallel point about knowledge: past events are inferred from present evidence rather than directly observed. A description can be structurally faithful without being ontologically identical to what it describes. A map can accurately describe a city without containing the city, and a mathematical model can represent a physical system without constituting another instance of that system. Three things must therefore be distinguished: referent ≠ representation ≠ instantiation They can correspond without being identical. 2. Information is relational Consider a rock that is photographed. There is the rock; the electromagnetic interaction producing the image; the physical image; the information it encodes; the observer’s interpretation; and the resulting proposition. Calling all six “the information” obscures important distinctions. Information can be understood as involving relations between physical states and the distinctions they encode or make available. The same informational content can have different physical encodings: “HELLO” can be represented through ink, voltages, sound waves, magnetic domains, pixels, or neural activity. Destroying one representation need not eliminate that information if other encodings remain. Destroying every accessible encoding may make the information permanently unrecoverable, although the corresponding fact need not cease to be true. Truth, representation, and accessibility are separate matters. 3. Knowledge depends on mediated access The signal-based epistemology replaces “thing → knowledge” with a mediated chain: thing → signal → detection → interpretation → knowledge An observer does not simply possess reality. It processes physically available information and develops an understanding through interpretation and inference. Ignorance is not necessarily an intellectual failure; physical reality can impose limits on available information. The framework identifies logical, physical, computational, and interpretive limits on knowledge. It defines knowledge as requiring sufficient confirmation while acknowledging that some truths cannot be verified. Two principles follow: \* A fact can be true without any observer being able to establish it. \* A confident belief can exist without its content being true. Confirmation is not the same as correctly interpreting what was confirmed This is a subtle but powerful principle. Suppose a child sees the Moon moving across the sky and concludes that the Moon is flying around Earth like a bird. The child really did observe the Moon appearing at different locations over time. But the conclusion that the Moon is flying like a bird goes beyond what the observation itself establishes. Or suppose a child notices that their shadow follows them wherever they go and concludes that the shadow likes to follow them. Again, the observation is real. The interpretation is not thereby established. This gives us a useful distinction: Observing something, correctly identifying what was observed, and correctly explaining why it occurred are three different achievements that are not guaranteed to be achieved. Even when an observation is accurate, the explanation can be mistaken. And even when an explanation is plausible, it can remain unconfirmed. This matters because people often describe themselves as having confirmed an entire explanation when they have actually confirmed only some of its components. "For example: 'You observe a correlation, but that doesn’t establish causation. You observe behavior, but that doesn’t establish the exact intention behind it. You observe a physical representation, but that doesn’t establish that the representation is identical to what it represents. You observe that a claim is widely accepted, but that doesn’t establish its truth.'" The deeper lesson is that the evidential support for a conclusion cannot automatically exceed what the evidence and valid reasoning actually establish. This doesn’t mean conclusions can never go beyond direct observation. Scientific reasoning routinely establishes things indirectly. It means that every additional inference needs appropriate justification. Perhaps one of the most consequential intellectual achievements isn’t discovering a new fact, but learning to recognize exactly which fact has been established, which conclusion follows from it, and which additional conclusion has not yet been earned. That skill doesn’t guarantee that we’ll discover the truth. It does help us avoid mistaking our interpretations, representations, and assumptions for the truth itself. The world does not become more or less true according to how confidently we understand it. Our responsibility is to make our understanding answer to the world—not to make the world answer to our understanding. 4. Epistemic limits do not imply ontological indeterminacy Suppose an event occurs and leaves no surviving detectable trace. “We cannot establish whether X happened” and “There is no fact about whether X happened” are different claims. The signal-based framework keeps these apart. It allows that some truths may remain permanently inaccessible. The boundary of knowledge need not be the boundary of reality. This works both ways: inability to detect X does not establish that X does not exist, while the possibility that X exists does not justify believing that it does. Three epistemic conditions are worth distinguishing: \* Known: sufficient confirming evidence is available. \* Currently unknown: evidence might become available. \* Structurally unknowable: the information required for confirmation cannot become available to the relevant observer or system. Ignorance can therefore be temporary, permanent, or imposed by fundamental constraints. 5. One reality, many information channels Observers differ in location, senses, history, computational capacity, causal access, and interpretive models. Consequently, they may possess different information about the same reality. This does not require separate realities for separate observers. Differences in perception and belief can arise from differences in evidence, processing, and interpretation. The position is neither naive realism nor the claim that everything is subjective: reality can be observer-independent while access to it remains perspectival and limited. 6. Coherence versus contact A coherent model is internally consistent; an empirically successful model also makes contact with reality through observation, prediction, and testing. Coherence alone does not establish truth. A logically consistent explanation can be wrong if its claims fail to correspond to the world. The signal-based framework also describes useful explanations as portable “loop-packages”: representations that other minds can apply, test, refine, and use to support prediction or intervention. Knowledge, on this approach, is not possession of a complete picture of reality. It is a sufficiently confirmed understanding developed through reliable interactions with available evidence. Science accordingly involves constructing, testing, and improving models rather than merely accumulating descriptions. 7. Reality exceeds our models If reality were identical to an observer’s current model, discovery and error would lose their ordinary distinction. Reality can surprise us precisely because our representations can be incomplete or mistaken. Consider the progression: causal access → information → representation → reconstruction → identity None of these transitions is automatic. Detecting a star does not mean describing it completely. Describing something does not mean physically reproducing it. Reproducing information about a person does not, by itself, establish that the person has been reproduced as the same individual. The distinction between representation and instantiation is essential. PART II. Causation, Time, and Becoming 8. The present as the place where reality is generated Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP) proposes that only the currently generated state of the universe exists. Earlier states do not persist as independently real regions of spacetime. What ceases to exist is each earlier state itself; the systems that were in those states may persist, subject to their own conditions of persistence. The present physical state instead bears causal consequences of earlier events in its matter, fields, radiation, and other structures, some of which function as records. Present systems exhibit lawful physical continuity and may undergo transformations without preserving earlier states themselves. The present is not merely whichever coordinate is labeled “now.” Under CTGP, it has ontological significance as the currently generated reality. A memory of yesterday exists presently; yesterday does not thereby exist again. Radiation detected from a distant event exists presently; the earlier configuration responsible for its emission does not. The past and future are asymmetric. Earlier events really occurred but no longer exist as events occurring. Future events have not yet been generated. The future is therefore not merely unknown; under CTGP, it does not yet exist. 9. What makes truths about the past true Presentism faces a truthmaker problem: if earlier states no longer exist, what makes statements about them true? CTGP adopts a Lucretian approach, associated with Bigelow (1996). The presently existing world instantiates primitive past-tensed properties expressing that particular things occurred at earlier stages. These properties are attributed to the present world and, once acquired, are retained at subsequent stages. Physical records serve a different function. They provide evidence of earlier events and can be damaged, altered, or erased. They are not themselves the fundamental truthmakers. Erasing a record does not change what happened. It changes what observers can establish about what happened. CTGP accepts a metaphysical cost: primitive past-tensed properties need not supervene on the present categorical physical configuration. Two worlds could be categorically alike at a given stage yet have different histories. This invites the familiar objection that Lucretian properties are metaphysically unilluminating or “cheat,” associated with critics such as Sider. The central distinction remains: What happened is not identical to what can presently be established about what happened. 10. Causal influence, surviving information, and recoverability Suppose A causes B, B causes C, and C causes D. When D exists, A may no longer exist, and no observer may be able to reconstruct A from surviving evidence. Nevertheless, D may have its particular properties partly because A occurred. Three notions must remain distinct: causal influence ≠ surviving information ≠ recoverability Loss of recoverable information about an earlier cause does not erase causal consequences that have already occurred. For example, someone reads a letter, makes a decision, and burns the letter. Its wording may eventually become unrecoverable, but the decision may already have affected other people and events. Even the reader’s memory could disappear without undoing those consequences. This does not mean information loss is always causally irrelevant. Erasing information can change a system’s subsequent behavior. The point is that losing evidence of an earlier cause does not retroactively undo its effects. Under CTGP, present physical reality may bear consequences of earlier events without containing enough information to reconstruct those events. A present consequence is not necessarily a record of its cause. 11. The present physical basis of future causation CTGP’s generability commitment requires the fundamental laws to determine, deterministically or probabilistically, the transition from the present state to subsequent states without independently imposed future boundary conditions, final-state conditions, or global consistency requirements spanning the whole history. CTGP further restricts attempts to enlarge the state space: additional dynamical variables must be physically instantiated in the present and possess appropriate dynamics. A central principle follows within the framework: Whatever present or future effects depend on earlier events must be generated through physically instantiated present conditions and the lawful dynamics acting on them. Earlier states cannot perform new causal work after ceasing to exist. If two present states agree in every categorical physical respect, CTGP requires what comes next for each to be governed by the same laws and transition probabilities, regardless of differences in past-tensed truth profiles that have no dynamical role. This does not require observers to decode every causally relevant feature. Present physical conditions may be distributed, complex, scrambled, or computationally inaccessible. Physical causal relevance is not identical to recoverable historical information. This is a theoretical commitment of CTGP, not an independently established result about every possible fundamental theory. 12. Causality is lawful dependence, not foreknowledge A cause need not know its effect. A neuron need not represent the decision in which it participates. A weather system need not contain a forecast of tomorrow’s weather. Under CTGP, the present need not contain an already existing future to generate subsequent states. Causality is lawful dependence, not foreknowledge. Predictability and causation are distinct. A process can be causally determined yet computationally unpredictable, while a reliable prediction does not necessarily cause what it predicts. Likewise, an outcome’s definiteness when realized does not establish that it existed before its realization. Three concepts must be distinguished: prewritten ≠ lawful ≠ predictable Lawfulness does not necessarily imply that outcomes are predetermined, and unpredictability does not imply absence of causal structure. CTGP allows lawful generation without an already existing future. Whether a particular generative law is deterministic or stochastic is a separate question. A completed causal sequence can be reconstructed retrospectively even when no earlier participant possessed a complete representation of its outcome. 13. A complete causal history without a complete history book Suppose a lawful sequence runs from A to E, but afterward all evidence distinguishing A from alternative antecedents disappears. There may still be a determinate fact about what happened, even if no observer can reconstruct it. The signal-based epistemology allows this distinction: \* Causal completeness does not entail epistemic completeness. \* Intelligibility does not guarantee reconstructibility. \* A lawful reality can be informationally lossy. Even where information survives, a further limitation arises: an observer modeling the universe is itself part of the universe. A perfectly complete internal model would face demanding requirements involving physical resources, self-reference, and representation of its own modeling activity. These create obstacles to obtaining a complete “view from nowhere,” although they do not alone prove that every conceivable form of complete knowledge is impossible. Reality can be determinate without furnishing observers with a complete recoverable account of its history. PART III. Agency and the Representation of the Future 14. Agency does not require exemption from physics CTGP can accommodate conscious agency, where it exists, as a physically instantiated causal process rather than an extra-physical power that selects outcomes independently of physical laws. Thus: agency ≠ exemption from causality An agent can be physically constituted, influenced by earlier conditions, capable of deliberation, responsive to reasons, and causally effective. Reasoning, memories, values, emotions, interpretations, and decisions can participate in the processes producing behavior. Being causally influenced does not mean one’s reasoning has no causal role. The signal-based framework similarly allows creative thought to develop through ongoing recombination, evaluation, feedback, and modification rather than through the revelation of a fully formed idea existing beforehand. New configurations can arise through lawful physical processes. 15. Memories and plans have different causal roles Under CTGP, memories and plans are presently existing physical and mental states representing events at other times. Their causal relationships differ. A memory ordinarily arises through an appropriate causal connection with an earlier experience. Its accuracy depends on correspondence with what happened, while its status as a genuine memory also depends on the relevant connection between the experience and the present memory. A plan represents a possible future and may help bring about what it represents. Thus: \* Memory: earlier experience → present representation. \* Prediction: present representation → possible later correspondence. \* Intention: present representation → action → possible later outcome. A forecast succeeds by accurately describing what occurs. An intention can succeed partly because it influences what occurs. This suggests a functional description of agency: An agent is a causal system whose representations of possible future states can participate in determining its subsequent behavior and outcomes. Nothing requires future events to exist already. A presently existing representation of a possible future can have causal effects without the represented future being real. 16. Representing alternatives does not require existing alternative futures An intelligent system can consider alternatives: “I could do A or B. If I do A, C may follow; if I do B, D may follow.” The represented alternatives exist as present informational or cognitive states. The alternative future events themselves need not exist. Therefore: possible future ≠ existing future Yet a representation of a possible future can be part of present causal reality. Plans, fears, forecasts, simulations, and contingency models can influence behavior. This permits agents to respond causally to possibilities without those possibilities already being actual. The same general kind of physical structure—an information-bearing representation—can have different causal roles depending on its origin and effects. A photograph of a fire and a plan to start a fire may represent similar events while functioning very differently. PART IV. Personal Identity 17. Identity as continuity of one’s own mind One candidate account of personal identity emphasizes the continuity of an individual’s mind as an ongoing causal and psychological process. This does not require retaining the same atoms, bodily configuration, or mental contents throughout life. Molecules are replaced, memories change, and neural activity varies. A person can undergo substantial physical and psychological alteration while remaining the same individual under ordinary identity judgments. The proposed criterion is: Personal identity consists in the continuity of one’s own mind as an ongoing causal and psychological process, rather than persistence of a particular material configuration. Here, “mind” cannot mean only the thoughts currently experienced. Sleep, anesthesia, temporary unconsciousness, and memory loss need not terminate personal identity. Three forms of continuity are relevant: \* Mental continuity: persistence of the individual’s organized psychological capacities and processes. \* Memory continuity: preservation of and access to aspects of accumulated experience. \* Causal continuity: the actual physical and psychological development connecting earlier and later stages. These relationships are important, but their precise sufficiency for numerical identity remains philosophically contested. 18. Identity under presentism Under CTGP, a person can persist while their earlier states do not: what no longer exists is the state they were in ten years ago, not necessarily the person. Saying “I am the person I was ten years ago” claims that the presently existing individual is the one who was in that earlier state. CTGP grounds the relevant past-tense truth in the present world’s primitive past-tensed properties. Present memories, neural organization, habits, dispositions, and commitments may also provide physical evidence of causal continuity. Memory does not create the historical fact of identity. Nor does remembering an event automatically establish that the rememberer personally experienced it. The proposed account emphasizes that the person’s present state is a causal continuation of their earlier states, while recognizing that causal continuation alone does not settle every question about numerical identity. 19. Three kinds of persistence persistence of organization ≠ persistence of information ≠ persistence of experience An organized system may persist while its constituent materials change. Information may survive the destruction of its original encoding if another encoding exists. An experience may cease even though information describing it remains available. These distinctions become especially important in hypothetical cases involving copying, reconstruction, and consciousness. 20. Duplication: information is not numerical identity Imagine producing a second human with the original person’s relevant brain structure, memories, personality, and cognitive dispositions. The duplicate sincerely believes it has lived the original person’s life. Nevertheless, the two are physically distinct individuals after duplication. Thus: same information ≠ same causal history ≠ numerical identity The duplicate’s memories are not necessarily causally disconnected from the original experiences. Their causal ancestry may run through the original brain, scanning equipment, stored information, and reconstruction. But causal ancestry alone does not establish numerical identity. A child is causally descended from a parent without being that parent. An identity theory must specify which causal relationships constitute continuation of an individual rather than production of another. The duplicate need not be considered unreal or inferior. If conscious, it would be a real individual whose psychological state includes information derived from another person’s experiences. 21. Branching and psychological continuity Suppose one person produces two psychologically continuous successors, each possessing the original memories and personality. If psychological continuity alone guaranteed numerical identity, both successors would be identical to the original. By transitivity, they would then be identical to each other, despite being two distinct individuals. This is the fission problem associated with Derek Parfit. It demonstrates that unrestricted psychological continuity cannot, by itself, serve as a sufficient criterion of numerical identity. The quantum no-cloning theorem prohibits perfect copying of arbitrary unknown quantum states. It does not, by itself, establish that producing functionally or psychologically similar successors is impossible. Whether such duplication could ever be technologically achieved remains unknown. Under CTGP, each successor would possess its own present physical state and causal history. Records might establish which successor underwent which procedure, but the numerical-identity question still requires a theory of persistence. Even if every distinguishing record disappeared, that would not automatically erase the historical facts. 22. Transformation versus reconstruction Continuity of activity is not identical to continuity of identity. During sleep or anesthesia, conscious experience may be absent or altered while the brain’s organized physical structure remains. Temporary suppression of detectable neural activity need not imply destruction of the individual. Ordinary biological replacement involves ongoing physical organization participating in its own maintenance. Scan-and-rebuild procedures introduce a different causal pathway: a system is measured, information is encoded, and another system is constructed from that representation. Consider a city reconstructed elsewhere from detailed plans. The new city may closely resemble the original without thereby being numerically identical to it. Similarly, reconstructing someone’s physical and psychological organization does not automatically establish preservation of that person’s numerical identity. The distinction motivates a candidate criterion: Personal identity consists in uninterrupted causal inheritance of an embodied organization, rather than reconstruction from a representation. This is a philosophical proposal, not an established theorem of physics. If adopted, it suggests: \* Sleep and anesthesia with continuing physical organization: identity preserved. \* Gradual biological replacement within an ongoing organism: identity preserved. \* Gradual synthetic replacement integrated into that organization: plausibly preserved. \* Scan, destruction, and reconstruction elsewhere: identity not preserved under this criterion. \* Reconstruction while the original survives: two distinct individuals. \* Copying from stored information: creation of another individual. \* Gradual repair of the Ship of Theseus: plausibly preservation of the original ship. Difficult cases remain. Replacing neurons individually with functional equivalents could be understood as continuing an organization or as successive local reconstructions. Potentially relevant features include locality, temporal continuity, preservation of causal organization, informational bottlenecks, and branching. The framework has not established which combination is decisive. It identifies the unresolved identity conditions. 23. Is the identity criterion discovered or chosen? Some disagreements concern unknown empirical facts. Others concern which criteria should determine the application of a concept. Personal identity may involve both. Physics could describe a reconstruction procedure in extraordinary detail without independently settling whether its result counts as the same person. That depends partly on the theory of persistence being applied. This does not prove that identity is merely conventional. It leaves open whether reality determines a unique identity relation or whether competing criteria capture different legitimate concerns. The central conclusion is: Preserving information about an individual does not, by itself, establish preservation of that individual’s numerical identity. A theory of identity must explain what kind of continuity, if any, makes a later individual the same person. PART V. Human Duplication as a Future Technology 24. Clones and duplicates A biological clone is primarily a genetic copy, not a copy of an existing person’s memories and personality. A cloned human, if reproductive cloning became feasible, would ordinarily develop from an embryo rather than emerge as an adult duplicate. Mammalian cloning has been demonstrated since Dolly the sheep’s birth in 1996, and cloned macaques were reported in 2018. Human reproductive cloning is not an established technology and raises substantial safety and ethical concerns. A duplicate possessing someone’s detailed physical and mental organization would be a much more demanding achievement. 25. Why full duplication remains remote The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons and an estimated hundreds of trillions of synaptic connections, depending on counting methods. Reconstructing a person’s memories and cognitive organization would likely require much more than a simple wiring diagram, including relevant synaptic strengths, molecular states, and other physiological features. A complete adult fruit-fly brain connectome, involving roughly 140,000 neurons and about 50 million synapses, was published in 2024 after extensive collaborative work. Current high-resolution connectomic methods generally require invasive tissue preparation and are not suitable for nondestructively scanning a living human brain at comparable detail. Being in the universe is not being the universe The relation “is part of” must be distinguished from “is identical to.” A teapot in a kitchen occupies part of a house and interacts with its surroundings, but it is not the house. Likewise, humans are physically constituted within the universe, depend on its processes, and interact with other parts of it. That does not make any individual human numerically identical to the universe as a whole. Being part of reality does not mean being identical to all of reality. With “interaction” meaning signals being made available, received, and/or acted upon within “the universe as a whole”, can whole-universe states’ things/entities physically directly interact from distinct states, not just only be causally connected? When it comes to experimentally established physics and mathematical descriptions that successfully account for observations, no direct physical interaction between entities as instantiated in distinct whole-universe states has been demonstrated, established, and/or required by the evidence. That doesn’t mean such interaction has been proved impossible under every conceivable physical theory. There’s an important difference between what established physics describes, what a particular theory excludes by construction, and what we can conclude about the universe itself. I think the first thing to distinguish is physical interaction from causal connection. By direct physical interaction, I mean a physical coupling between specified entities, rather than merely a causal relationship established through intermediate physical carriers or processes. I’m not assuming in advance that direct coupling must occur at a single spacetime event, because that would partly settle the question by definition. Take light emitted by a distant star and subsequently detected here. The detector interacts with the electromagnetic field physically available at the detection event. The star’s earlier emission is causally relevant, but that doesn’t establish that the detector directly interacts with the star as it was at the earlier stage. In the standard description, the field mediates the relationship. The detector doesn’t need the star’s earlier configuration to exist alongside it for the detection to occur. This also matters for what I mean by signals being made available, received, and/or acted upon. Making a signal available doesn’t automatically mean interacting with some particular future recipient, and receiving one doesn’t automatically mean directly interacting with its original source. Those are physical activities involving signals, but whether two specified entities directly interact is a further question. Requiring all three activities, or allowing any one of them, doesn’t change that distinction. In standard local relativistic quantum field theory, the locality of interactions is part of the mathematical structure. Interaction terms are built from fields and, where applicable, their derivatives evaluated at the same spacetime point. A simple example is: L_int(x) = g φ₁(x) φ₂(x) φ₃(x) The fields in that expression are coupled at x, not through a fundamental interaction term directly joining independently specified events at different times. There’s also microcausality, which for suitable local observables requires: [A(x), B(y)] = 0 when x and y are spacelike separated. But that shouldn’t be confused with a general prohibition on every conceivable direct interaction between different temporal stages. Events on different hypersurfaces of a foliation can be spacelike, lightlike, or timelike related, and microcausality concerns only the spacelike case; the locality of the interaction terms is the more relevant point here. So, within standard local field theory, if we additionally define direct interaction as local physical coupling at a common spacetime event, entities instantiated exclusively at distinct global stages cannot directly interact as those distinct temporal instantiations. That’s a consequence of the definition and the theory’s local structure, not an independent experimental proof that every physically possible theory must work that way. An extended physical process can involve events at many different times, but that doesn’t make each local coupling an interaction between different whole-universe states. There’s another complication: what exactly counts as a whole-universe state? Relativity allows physical states to be specified on suitable spacelike hypersurfaces, but its fundamental laws don’t generally select one uniquely preferred succession of them. A particular spacetime geometry can distinguish a foliation, though. For example, where cosmological time is regular, its level sets provide geometrically distinguished Cauchy hypersurfaces under the relevant conditions. That distinction matters because a geometrically distinguished foliation isn’t automatically an ontologically privileged present. The geometry can supply the slicing without establishing that only one slice exists, or that all slices coexist. Those are additional claims about temporal existence. And regardless of which suitable foliation is chosen, a pointlike local interaction event belongs to one hypersurface of that foliation, even though a larger physical process can involve events on many hypersurfaces. Now, there are serious theoretical formulations that complicate any categorical claim that physical interactions must always be local in this particular sense. Wheeler–Feynman direct-action electrodynamics is an example. Rather than treating an independently propagating electromagnetic field as the fundamental mediator, it formulates interactions through direct relationships between charged particles at lightlike-separated events, with time-symmetric contributions associated with source events in both the past and future light cones. Schematically, its direct-action structure includes terms of the form: S_int ∼ Σ_{i≠j} ∫ dτᵢ dτⱼ (ẋᵢ · ẋⱼ) δ((xᵢ − xⱼ)²) Here the delta function restricts the coupled events to lightlike separation. Unlike the local interaction-density example, this term relates variables associated with different spacetime events. So I wouldn’t dismiss it as merely ordinary local field theory with different bookkeeping. The mathematical coupling really is nonlocal in its direct-action formulation. But I also wouldn’t infer from that mathematics that distinct whole-universe states physically coexist or directly interact as independently existing wholes. A relationship between charged-particle events isn’t automatically a relationship between entire global states as separate physical entities. Nor has Wheeler–Feynman theory been experimentally established as an indispensable fundamental replacement for conventional electromagnetic field theory. The appearance of advanced terms or future-absorber conditions also doesn’t, by itself, demonstrate that independent future boundary information is physically indispensable in every adequate account of the observations. I think the same caution applies more generally. A mathematical relationship between variables at different times, a physical interaction between specified entities, and an ontological claim that distinct temporal states coexist are three different propositions. Even if an experiment eventually established an indispensable direct coupling between temporally separated events, that wouldn’t automatically prove eternalism, or that whole-universe states themselves interact. It would first establish something about the dynamics of the physical degrees of freedom involved. There’s also no need to treat different configurations of a persisting entity as numerically distinct entities. A person, planet, particle, or field can be described at successive stages without assuming that its earlier and later configurations are separate objects interacting with one another. How persistence is understood is a further philosophical question, and neither presentism nor eternalism is uniquely established by the local interactions and causal connections we observe. So I wouldn’t answer with an unconditional “no, it’s impossible,” because that would claim more than the evidence establishes. But I also wouldn’t answer “yes” merely because physical processes connect events at different times, or because some mathematical formulations directly couple temporally separated variables. The conclusion I think the evidence supports is that local physical interactions and causal connections across time are established, whereas direct physical interaction between entities as instantiated in distinct whole-universe states has not been demonstrated, established, nor required by the evidence. Standard local relativistic field theories account for the relevant observations without requiring such cross-stage direct coupling. Alternative formulations show why universal impossibility shouldn’t simply be assumed, but they don’t establish that the universe actually contains independently interacting global temporal states. In other words, being causally connected across different times isn’t the same as directly interacting from those different times, and neither relationship by itself tells us whether past, present, and future coexist. According to general relativity, could someone light-years away take only 5 seconds of their own time to speak a sentence while years pass here before they finish speaking, even though both locations exist in the same universe progressing through time? Established relativistic physics: Different worldlines (the paths objects take through spacetime) can accumulate dramatically different amounts of proper time between appropriately specified events. Someone could experience only 5 seconds while speaking a sentence, whereas years elapse on another person’s clock over a corresponding interval. Distance alone doesn’t cause this; relativistic motion and gravitational conditions can. Cosmic-Time Generative Presentism (CTGP), adds an ontological interpretation: Only the currently generated state of the universe exists. Earlier states no longer exist as states, and future states have not yet been generated. CTGP identifies the present with a cosmological-time hypersurface: a three-dimensional spatial slice of spacetime whose points share the same value of cosmological time. Cosmological time is defined as the supremum of proper time along past-directed causal curves from each event, roughly corresponding, in an idealized expanding universe, to the greatest elapsed proper time since the Big Bang. Where cosmological time is regular, these hypersurfaces are future Cauchy surfaces: every worldline that continues past one crosses it exactly once. CTGP interprets each such surface as one objectively existing present, making all its locations co-present, even when separated by light-years. As successive hypersurfaces are generated, different observers can accumulate very different amounts of local proper time. Neither observer’s clock determines the progression of the shared present. In short: Relativity permits different elapsed local times; CTGP interprets the corresponding events as belonging to one succession of objectively generated presents. The latter is a philosophical interpretation, not something general relativity independently establishes. A block contains organizational structures (hurricanes, brains, evaluative architectures) as real patterns. Interventions at the level of intentions reliably change behavior; that counterfactual structure is available in a block as relations among possible histories. In a block, the agent did not create its own architecture ex nihilo; it performs the relevant computation, evaluation, prediction, and behavioral integration at its own time. Predictive processing does not require an unsettled future. In a block, the brain’s forward models are present-state structures that bias subsequent processing. Those models and their alignment with outcomes are part of the fixed history. The organism is not “predicting a future that could be otherwise”; it is generating anticipatory states that causally guide behavior. The felt sense of control arises when predictions and outcomes align, whether the block is deterministic or stochastic. “Decisions unfold only in the present moment” is indexical, not ontological. Things can sometimes be identified at the macroscopic level by their organization, continuity, or identity-defining characteristics, rather than by every individual constituent at lower scales. This means they don’t necessarily need to retain every single characteristic to remain the same entity. Therefore, a thing can remain what it is as long as the characteristics essential to its continued identity are preserved, but that doesn’t mean the thing hasn’t changed in other respects. For example, a hurricane can be understood as a persisting macroscopic pattern or organized physical system, even while its constituent molecules, location, intensity, and structure change. Likewise, an organism can remain the same individual while undergoing physical changes. So I’d distinguish an entity remaining numerically the same from remaining qualitatively unchanged. An entity doesn’t necessarily need to retain every characteristic to remain itself; only whatever conditions are necessary for its continued identity. Regarding the philosophical distinction between essential and nonessential properties, some properties may be necessary for an entity to remain what it is, while others can change without terminating its identity. For example, a human can change height, location, age, and cellular composition without necessarily ceasing to be the same individual. However, I’d distinguish the properties necessary for being a particular kind of entity from the conditions necessary for remaining the same individual. Those conditions may also involve causal or historical continuity, rather than just an unchanging set of properties. So I’m not claiming that every entity necessarily has a universally agreed-upon division of properties. I’m saying that changing a property doesn’t automatically entail losing numerical identity. Can a physical theory in principle derive a definite underlying event history while allowing some effective quantum operations to lack a definite order or allowing operational descriptions that assign no single classical ordering to those operations? Yes, in principle, in suitable theoretical settings. A definite underlying event history and an indefinite operational causal order are not necessarily contradictory. The crucial distinction is between the physical events that actually occur and the effective quantum operations used to describe an experiment. In nonrelativistic settings, single-outcome theories such as Bohmian mechanics and GRW-type collapse theories already derive definite trajectories or events from their dynamics while retaining quantum interference (section 3). However, these examples do not, by themselves, establish a complete relativistic theory deriving a single realized history for all operational indefinite-causal-order phenomena. 1. What indefinite causal order (ICO) experiments establish In a quantum switch, two operations, A and B, can be coherently controlled so that the experiment cannot generally be represented as an ordinary probabilistic mixture of the fixed orders A-before-B and B-before-A. Interference between these alternatives can produce operational statistics incompatible with such a mixture. This is evidence of causal nonseparability at the level of the effective operations. However, causal nonseparability does not, by itself, establish that every underlying physical event lacks a definite causal relationship or that spacetime itself has no definite causal structure. Also, the standard quantum switch does not, by itself, violate a causal inequality. Causal nonseparability and causal-inequality violation are distinct properties. Recent device-independent tests certify the switch's indefinite order in a different way: they add a spacelike-separated party entangled with the switch and assume relativistic causality and free interventions, a Bell-type scenario rather than a standard causal inequality (van der Lugt, Barrett, and Chiribella 2023). Photonic experiments along these lines have been reported (Richter et al. 2026; Qu et al. 2025), with loopholes still open. 2. How a definite underlying history can coexist with operational ICO Consider a quantum-switch experiment implemented using a coherent superposition of different paths through physical devices. At the effective level, operations A and B have no single classical order that adequately describes the process. At the more detailed physical level, however, the apparatus consists of interactions occurring in spacetime. A theory may assign definite causal relationships to these localized interactions while retaining the quantum coherence responsible for the operational statistics. Vilasini and Renner (2024) established an important result: under their assumptions, implementations of indefinite-order processes in a fixed classical spacetime that respect relativistic causality admit a finer-grained description with definite, acyclic causal order. This demonstrates that operational indefinite order can be compatible with a finer-grained, definite causal structure under those assumptions. It does not, by itself, establish a single objectively realized event history. The distinction matters because an effective operation is not necessarily identical to one uniquely localized spacetime event. It may represent a transformation implemented through multiple physical interactions or delocalized quantum degrees of freedom. Consequently, an underlying description with definite spacetime causal relationships need not imply that the effective operations admit a classical mixture of definite orders. This is not the same as saying that each run behaves like an A-before-B or a B-before-A circuit, with ordinary classical probabilities. That simpler explanation fails to reproduce quantum-switch interference, even in theories where some underlying variable, such as a particle’s path, does take one route. 3. Examples from single-outcome quantum theories Two existing approaches illustrate how single-outcome ontologies can accommodate quantum interference without necessarily assigning a unique classical ordering to effective operations. Bohmian mechanics In Bohmian mechanics, particles have definite trajectories, while the guiding wave evolves through coherent alternatives. Consider a quantum switch implemented using a massive particle whose spatial path controls whether it encounters operation A or B first. Before the paths recombine, the particle follows one actual route, but the interference observed at the output depends on the guiding wave associated with both routes. Bohmian mechanics reproduces the standard quantum predictions for output-position measurements under quantum equilibrium. Thus, a definite underlying trajectory does not make the experiment equivalent to an ordinary classical mixture of definite-order circuits. GRW-type collapse theories GRW-type collapse theories provide another approach. In the flash ontology, the realized history consists of definite spacetime events called flashes. For a short experiment involving a microscopic particle, spontaneous collapses are extraordinarily unlikely, so quantum-switch interference can survive essentially unchanged. If no collapse occurs during the coherent evolution, the collapse dynamics does not select either effective operational-order branch as uniquely realized. In the flash ontology, the history need not contain an event identifying one of those branches as the actual one. This does not mean that the history establishes that neither operation came first, or that the underlying localized interactions lack definite causal relationships. It means that a definite history of flashes need not determine a unique classical ordering of the effective operations. These examples distinguish definite realized events from definite classical orderings of effective operations. They illustrate possible single-outcome accounts, while a fully satisfactory relativistic implementation remains a further theoretical requirement. 4. What a single-outcome theory must demonstrate Merely postulating one realized history is insufficient. A satisfactory theory must provide a consistent physical account of: How the underlying events and interactions are realized. How quantum coherence between alternative operational orders is preserved. How the observed interference and causal-nonseparability statistics arise. How the account remains consistent with relevant relativistic constraints and operational no-signaling. Why changing the effective description or quantum reference frame does not change which underlying history actually occurred. These are substantive mathematical and physical requirements, not automatic consequences of single-outcome realism. A definite history also need not mean that every pair of events is temporally ordered. In relativity, spacelike-separated events need not have an invariant before-and-after ordering. A history can be definite without possessing one universal total ordering of all events. 5. What remains unresolved Experiments support operational ICO in engineered quantum systems, but they do not establish that fundamentally indefinite spacetime causal order occurs naturally. Nor do they establish that a definite underlying history is impossible. Some interpretations and models permit definite underlying events or causal structures; others treat indefinite order as more fundamental. Determining which description, if any, reflects the ultimate ontology requires additional theoretical assumptions and potentially new empirical tests. In particular, demonstrating that a fixed-spacetime implementation reproduces quantum-switch statistics is not equivalent to constructing a complete, relativistically consistent, single-outcome theory of all quantum phenomena. 6. Bell’s theorem and Frauchiger–Renner Bell’s theorem rules out Bell-local explanations of certain quantum correlations under specified assumptions; it does not rule out every definite underlying event history. Nonlocal realist theories remain possible, although their compatibility with relativistic requirements raises additional challenges. Bell nonlocality must also be distinguished from operational signaling. In entangled systems, the joint correlations can violate Bell inequalities while the unconditioned local Born-rule statistics remain independent of the distant measurement setting. These are compatible facts: no-signaling does not eliminate the nonlocal correlations established by Bell experiments. Furthermore, Bell’s theorem does not, by itself, establish that every definite underlying history requires an invariant temporal ordering of spacelike-separated events or a Galilean spacetime structure. Certain realist models employ preferred foliations, but their necessity depends on additional theoretical assumptions. For example, Gisin (2011) established a no-go result for a class of deterministic nonlocal hidden-variable models required to reproduce quantum correlations independently of relativistic time ordering. By contrast, Tumulka (2006) constructed a Lorentz-invariant stochastic flash model for noninteracting distinguishable particles. This illustrates why the assumptions governing determinism, nonlocal dependence, and relativistic covariance matter. Neither result establishes that every definite-history theory requires a preferred frame, nor that every stochastic single-outcome theory automatically satisfies all relativistic requirements. Frauchiger–Renner addresses a different consistency problem involving quantum measurements and agents’ predictions. Its argument concerns the compatibility of particular assumptions about universal quantum reasoning, single outcomes, and consistency between agents’ conclusions. Although extended Wigner’s-friend scenarios have connections to Bell-type arguments, these connections do not make Frauchiger–Renner a general impossibility theorem against definite histories compatible with operational indefinite causal order. The central question remains whether a theory can derive a single realized history while reproducing the interference and causal-nonseparability statistics of effective quantum operations. Neither Bell’s theorem nor Frauchiger–Renner, by itself, settles that question. The crucial distinction is between definite underlying physical events and effective quantum operations whose statistics cannot be represented by a classical mixture of fixed orders. Bottom line Yes, in principle, in suitable theoretical settings. A definite underlying event history need not require every effective quantum operation to possess a single classical order. Operational indefinite causal order rules out particular classical mixtures of effective operation orders, not every possible definite underlying history. Nonrelativistic single-outcome theories provide mechanisms for definite trajectories or events to coexist with quantum interference. Results concerning finer-grained causal structures further support the distinction between underlying physical events and effective operation order. However, these results do not establish a complete relativistically satisfactory single-outcome theory reproducing all relevant indefinite-causal-order phenomena. Constructing such a theory remains an open challenge. When experiments demonstrate operational indefinite causal order (ICO), still never observed occurring naturally, is there objectively no fact about which operation came first, or can a definite underlying order exist that no observation can reveal? The answer depends on what we mean by “order.” There’s the order in which two quantum operations act on a system, the order of the physical interactions inside the apparatus, and the possibility of some deeper ordering that isn’t captured by the usual quantum description. Those aren’t necessarily the same thing. The experimental evidence for indefinite causal order (ICO) is real, but it doesn’t settle every one of those questions. What experiments support is that certain quantum processes cannot be represented as ordinary mixtures of processes in which A definitely precedes B or B definitely precedes A. That’s stronger than saying we don’t happen to know the order. It isn’t, by itself, a proof that reality contains no deeper fact about which interaction occurred first. So the experiments rule out an ordinary classical mixture of definite operation orders, not every possible deeper account in which events have a definite history. The standard example is the quantum switch. Imagine two quantum operations, A and B, acting on a target system initially in state |ψ⟩. A control qubit determines which operation comes first. If the control is |0⟩, A acts first and B second, giving BA|ψ⟩. If the control is |1⟩, B acts first and A second, giving AB|ψ⟩. The notation is right-to-left because the rightmost operator acts first. If the control is prepared in a superposition, (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, the combined output is (|0⟩ ⊗ BA|ψ⟩ + |1⟩ ⊗ AB|ψ⟩)/√2. The important thing is that this is a coherent superposition, not merely a situation where the experimenter doesn’t know which setting was chosen. Now measure the control in the basis |±⟩ = (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2. The probabilities are P(±) = ½[1 ± Re⟨BAψ|ABψ⟩]. That inner product measures the interference between the two possible orderings. For a particularly simple example, choose A = X and B = Z, the Pauli matrices. They anticommute: XZ = −ZX. Consequently, ⟨BAψ|ABψ⟩ = −1, and therefore P(−) = 1, P(+) = 0. The minus outcome occurs with certainty. If A and B commute instead, the plus outcome occurs with certainty. This is not something an ordinary classical random choice between the two orders reproduces. If the control is an incoherent mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩, measuring it in the |±⟩ basis gives P(+) = P(−) = ½. If both branches instead perform the same operation sequence coherently, the control remains |+⟩, giving P(−) = 0. Notice the qualification, though. These calculations distinguish particular coherent and incoherent implementations. They do not, by themselves, rule out every conceivable definite-order quantum circuit. The stronger result comes from the theory of quantum supermaps and quantum circuits: under specified restrictions, including a single use of each unknown operation, the quantum switch can accomplish discrimination tasks that circuits with a fixed order or a classical mixture of orders cannot accomplish with the same resources. Chiribella’s 2012 work on perfect discrimination and the 2013 paper by Chiribella, D’Ariano, Perinotti and Valiron establish results of this kind. So when physicists call the quantum switch causally nonseparable, they’re making a precise statement. Within the relevant operational framework, the process cannot be decomposed into a probabilistic mixture of an A-before-B process and a B-before-A process. That does not mean all conceivable underlying descriptions of the experiment have been ruled out. This distinction matters when asking whether there’s “objectively no fact” about the order. In the standard operational quantum description, the process has no definite order of the relevant operations in the sense just explained. It’s not an ordinary unknown classical variable selecting one of two process orders. But interpreting that as a statement about every possible underlying physical fact is an additional step. There is a useful way to see why ordinary ignorance is inadequate. Suppose the two alternatives leave distinguishable records in the environment. Let |e_BA⟩ be the environmental state associated with the A-then-B branch, and |e_AB⟩ the state associated with the B-then-A branch. The joint state becomes (|0⟩ ⊗ BA|ψ⟩ ⊗ |e_BA⟩ + |1⟩ ⊗ AB|ψ⟩ ⊗ |e_AB⟩)/√2. The interference probabilities are then P(±) = ½[1 ± Re(⟨BAψ|ABψ⟩⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩)]. The additional factor, ⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩, is the overlap between the environmental states associated with the two alternatives. If those states are identical, their overlap has magnitude one and they carry no distinguishable which-order information. The interference can remain intact. If the environmental states are orthogonal, ⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩ = 0, they are perfectly distinguishable. The interference term disappears, leaving P(+) = P(−) = ½. That’s decoherence. The environment has become correlated with the alternative orderings, and interference in the reduced system is lost. Nobody has to look at the environment for this to happen. The effect depends on physical entanglement and distinguishability, not on whether a human observer learns anything. There is a familiar quantitative relation here from Englert’s 1996 work on interference visibility and which-way information. For two equally weighted alternatives with pure environmental marker states, define the distinguishability as D = √(1 − |⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩|²). If V denotes the normalized interference visibility, then in the ideal pure-state case, V = |⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩|, and V² + D² = 1. More generally, the complementarity relation is V² + D² ≤ 1. This is important because it gives a physical meaning to the distinction between interference and accessible which-alternative information. As the alternatives become more distinguishable, the interference visibility decreases. It also explains why maintaining coherence becomes difficult when many environmental degrees of freedom are involved. Suppose the environmental state factors into N components and each component acquires a tiny amount of information distinguishing the alternatives. The total overlap is ⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩ = ∏ₖ ⟨eₖ,BA|eₖ,AB⟩. If each factor is approximately 1 − ε, then ⟨e_BA|e_AB⟩ ≈ (1 − ε)ᴺ ≈ e^(−Nε). Take ε = 10⁻⁶ and N = 10⁸. Then e^(−Nε) = e^(−100) ≈ 3.7 × 10⁻⁴⁴. Each environmental component carries very little distinguishing information, yet the combined effect almost completely suppresses interference. This is why simply saying “nobody measured the order” isn’t enough. Environmental correlations can matter even when nobody deliberately measures or collects them. But I would be careful about turning that into the claim that indefinite order requires absolutely no physical trace anywhere. The precise requirement is that distinguishability of the relevant alternatives must not destroy the coherence needed for the particular interference effect. Partial distinguishability can coexist with partial interference. Correlations may also be manipulated or erased under suitable conditions, and the result depends on which degrees of freedom are included and which measurements are performed. More importantly, the absence of a distinguishable quantum record does not logically establish the absence of every conceivable underlying fact. That’s a philosophical inference, not a consequence of the overlap equation alone. Consider the possibility that there really is some deeper variable O specifying which operation occurred first. If O is an ordinary classical variable that selects A-before-B or B-before-A and the experiment is otherwise described by the usual fixed-order processes, then the resulting process belongs to the class that causal-nonseparability tests are designed to exclude. Simply adding ignorance about O won’t reproduce the coherent quantum-switch statistics. But that doesn’t mean every hidden-order theory is impossible. A deeper theory might contain additional variables, nonclassical dynamics, or contextual relationships between the hidden description and observed results. Bohmian mechanics is an instructive comparison. In Bohmian mechanics, particles have definite trajectories, even when the wavefunction follows multiple paths and produces interference. The particle’s trajectory is not necessarily something that can be reconstructed from the ordinary quantum measurement statistics. One could investigate analogous trajectory-based descriptions of quantum-switch implementations, including descriptions in which a particle follows one definite route through the apparatus while the wavefunction has support on multiple routes. That would not automatically give us a universal hidden definite causal order for arbitrary quantum processes. It would establish something more limited: that a definite particle trajectory can coexist with operational interference between different orderings in a particular implementation. There is also a distinction between a hidden variable being inaccessible and being causally irrelevant. Suppose O is a hidden variable and R is some future measurement result. If the theory satisfies P(R | S, O) = P(R | S), where S is the complete operationally specified quantum state and experimental context, then knowing O adds no predictive information about R. But that condition is not automatically required of every possible hidden-variable theory. In a theory reproducing ordinary quantum predictions, hidden variables may influence individual outcomes even though their distribution leaves the observable statistics unchanged. Bohmian particle positions are an example of variables that are dynamically relevant within the theory but do not ordinarily permit predictions beyond quantum mechanics. In a theory like that, the hidden trajectory needn’t be meaningless or causally idle. It can play a genuine role in the underlying dynamics, even when ordinary measurements cannot determine it independently of the theory. Bohmian trajectory calculations can assign paths to particles in experiments such as the double slit, but those paths are theoretical reconstructions, not independently observed histories. If the theory reproduces standard quantum statistics, the agreement of those statistics alone cannot establish which ontology is correct. In fact, even if a result is certain, such as P(−) = 1 in the ideal anticommuting example, the fact that P(− | O) = 1 for every O with nonzero probability tells us only that this particular outcome is certain regardless of O. It doesn’t establish that O has no effect on any other measurement. Likewise, a Bell-like or theory-independent causal test excludes the class of models satisfying its assumptions. Depending on the test, these may include locality, free choice of interventions, particular laboratory isolation conditions, or a definite causal ordering. Violating the relevant inequality means that those assumptions cannot all be maintained together. It doesn’t uniquely identify which assumption must fail. This is one reason the distinction between device-dependent and device-independent evidence matters. Most early quantum-switch experiments relied on characterizing the optical devices and trusting a quantum description of their operation. Procopio and colleagues demonstrated a photonic superposition of gate orders in 2015. Later experiments investigated increasingly demanding ways of certifying the effect. Rubino and colleagues studied correlations involving entangled temporal orders, and more recent work has pursued certification using Bell-like or causal inequalities. Richter and colleagues in Vienna reported the first implementation of a device-independent protocol, published in PRX Quantum in 2026: they violated such an inequality with a photonic quantum switch, while leaving the locality, freedom-of-choice, and detection loopholes open, along with a “closed-laboratory” assumption that each operation acts only once. Qu and colleagues reported a further device-independent certification experiment in a 2025 preprint, using correlations between spacelike-separated photonic systems. They address the locality and freedom-of-choice loopholes but still rely on a fair-sampling assumption, which leaves the detection loophole open. These are significant developments, but “device-independent” doesn’t mean completely assumption-free. Such tests still depend on physical conditions that must be justified experimentally, and loopholes can remain. Nor does a violation of a causal inequality automatically mean that fundamental spacetime itself has no definite causal structure. It means the observed correlations are incompatible with the relevant class of causally ordered models under the test’s assumptions. The next distinction is probably the most important one for the original question. In an ordinary laboratory quantum switch, the physical apparatus exists in a spacetime with an ordinary causal structure. The photon’s route can be coherently controlled so that it encounters the devices in different orders, but the apparatus itself doesn’t require spacetime to lose its causal ordering. For example, imagine two possible routes. Along one route the photon encounters A at an earlier time t₁ and B at a later time t₂. Along the other route it encounters B at t₁ and A at t₂. The target operations are therefore associated with different spacetime regions depending on the route. We can represent the alternatives schematically as Route 1: A(t₁) → B(t₂) Route 2: B(t₁) → A(t₂), with t₁ < t₂. The coherent quantum process doesn’t simply choose one route randomly. Both alternatives contribute to the quantum state, and their relative phase can affect measurement probabilities. Nevertheless, the individual time-localized interactions can be described within an ordinary spacetime causal structure. This is closely related to a result by Vilasini and Renner, published in Physical Review Letters in 2024. They showed, under their assumptions about relativistic causality and realizations in classical background spacetime, that indefinite-order processes require systems that are not localized in spacetime in the ordinary way. They also showed that such realizations admit a finer-grained description with a definite, acyclic causal order. That is a substantial result. It means an operationally indefinite ordering of two effective operations can coexist with a definite causal ordering of the finer-grained spacetime interactions. The distinction isn’t merely philosophical wordplay. The effective operation A may be associated with different spacetime regions in different branches of the quantum process. Treating A as one operation localized at one fixed spacetime event would then be misleading. Oreshkov’s 2019 work on time-delocalized quantum subsystems and operations develops precisely this sort of idea. Paunković and Vojinović, in their 2020 paper on causal orders, quantum circuits and spacetime, discuss why it matters when interpreting experimental claims about indefinite causal order. The important point is that we have to specify what the supposed ordering relation is between. If we’re talking about effective operations acting on a quantum system, the order can be indefinite in the operational sense. If we’re talking about finer-grained physical interactions in a fixed spacetime, those interactions may have a definite causal organization. And if we’re talking about an even deeper ordering of physical reality, neither of those descriptions automatically settles whether such an ordering exists. So the two possibilities in the question aren’t necessarily mutually exclusive. There can be no definite operational order of A and B while a definite causal ordering exists in a finer-grained description of the apparatus. There’s another way to formulate the problem that doesn’t depend on photons traveling along different optical routes. It involves quantum clocks. Suppose two clocks, A and B, have a relative offset Δ = t_A − t_B. Let operation U_A occur when clock A reads τ_A, and operation U_B occur when clock B reads τ_B. Using clock A’s time coordinate, the second operation occurs at t_A = τ_B + Δ, whereas the first occurs at t_A = τ_A. The order therefore depends on the sign of τ_A − (τ_B + Δ). If the relative offset is a definite classical quantity, then for fixed intervention readings the order can be definite. But suppose the relative clock offset is in a coherent superposition of two distinguishable alternatives, δ₁ and δ₂, chosen so that the operations occur in opposite orders. Schematically, the initial state might be (|f_δ₁⟩ + |f_δ₂⟩)/√2 ⊗ |ψ⟩. After the controlled interactions, it becomes (|f_δ₁⟩ ⊗ U_AU_B|ψ⟩ + |f_δ₂⟩ ⊗ U_BU_A|ψ⟩)/√2. Here the labels are chosen so that δ₁ corresponds to U_B acting first and δ₂ to U_A acting first. Now suppose the two clock-offset alternatives are orthogonal and we measure only the target system, without accessing the clocks’ coherence. In this idealized model, tracing out the clocks removes the interference terms, so the target statistics are exactly those of an incoherent mixture: P(α) = ∫ dΔ |χ(Δ)|² P_Δ(α). Here χ(Δ) is the normalized clock-offset wavefunction and P_Δ(α) is the outcome probability for a definite offset Δ. The expression is a weighted average of definite-offset predictions. It doesn’t mean the full quantum state was an incoherent mixture; it means measurements restricted to the target cannot distinguish the two descriptions in this model. Coherent measurements involving the clocks can reveal the interference. If the two offset states are orthogonal and we measure the clock in the superposition basis |f_±⟩ = (|f_δ₁⟩ ± |f_δ₂⟩)/√2, then the corresponding probabilities are P(±) = ½[1 ± Re⟨U_AU_Bψ|U_BU_Aψ⟩]. Again, the interference term distinguishes a coherent superposition of alternatives from an incoherent mixture. For example, if the real part of the overlap is 0.854, then P(+) = ½(1 + 0.854) = 0.927. That numerical result is just an illustration of the formula, not a universal prediction about quantum clocks. There are several qualifications here. The clock states have to be defined consistently, the intervention model matters, and one must distinguish a genuine coherent measurement from merely reading out a classical clock value. The formula is an idealized two-branch model, not a general theorem covering every quantum-clock construction. This is relevant to quantum reference frames because different physical clocks need not supply identical descriptions of when an interaction occurs. Baumann and Lock investigated temporal localization and causality across quantum reference frames in 2026. Their analysis shows that consistent modeling of interventions across multiple clocks can require temporal delocalization, and that certain cases naturally lead to indefinite operational causal order. That still doesn’t mean different clocks literally create different objective histories. Nor does it prove that there’s one unique fundamental history. Those are further interpretive questions. It means that assuming every quantum-clock description can be reduced to one ordinary classical timeline may be too restrictive. Gravity makes the issue even more interesting. General relativity says that clocks tick at rates influenced by gravitational potential and spacetime geometry. If the source of the gravitational field is itself in a quantum superposition, one can investigate whether the relative timing of operations becomes correlated with different gravitational configurations. In a weak, approximately static gravitational field, the fractional proper-time difference associated with a gravitational-potential difference is approximately Δτ/τ ≈ ΔΦ/c². For a point mass M, the magnitude of the potential difference between distances r₁ and r₂ is |ΔΦ| = GM|1/r₁ − 1/r₂|. Therefore, |Δτ|/τ ≈ (GM/c²)|1/r₁ − 1/r₂|. To get an idea of the scale, imagine a 1 kg mass in a hypothetical superposition of configurations placing it 1 cm and 2 cm from a clock. Using G ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², we get |ΔΦ| ≈ (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(100 − 50) ≈ 3.34 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s². Dividing by c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s² gives |Δτ|/τ ≈ 3.7 × 10⁻²⁶. Over one second, the proper-time difference is therefore approximately |Δτ| ≈ 3.7 × 10⁻²⁶ s. That’s an extraordinarily small difference. If two operations are arranged so that their relative order depends on this gravitationally induced timing difference, the relevant timing must be controlled on a correspondingly demanding scale in this example. Of course, this isn’t a realistic experimental proposal. Maintaining a kilogram-scale object in the required spatial quantum superposition would be an enormous challenge, and the gravitational interaction, coherence, and clock dynamics would all have to be treated consistently. The calculation illustrates the difficulty of producing a gravitationally controlled change of operation order. It does not establish that nature cannot produce such effects or that every gravitational quantum-switch proposal requires exactly this timing precision. Environmental decoherence is one major obstacle. Joos and other researchers have studied how scattering by environmental particles and radiation can suppress coherence between spatially separated states. In simplified models, the off-diagonal coherence can decay approximately as ρ(x, x′; t) ≈ ρ(x, x′; 0)e^(−Λ(x − x′)²t), where Λ is a decoherence coefficient appropriate to the model and environment. Writing Δx = |x − x′|, the corresponding characteristic time is τ_dec ≈ 1/(ΛΔx²). To put numbers on it, Joos and Zeh estimated Λ for objects scattering the cosmic microwave background, which fills even the emptiest intergalactic space. For a dust grain about 10 micrometres across (a = 10⁻³ cm) they get roughly Λ ≈ 10⁶ cm⁻² s⁻¹, so for a separation equal to its size, Δx = 10⁻³ cm, τ_dec ≈ 1/[10⁶ × (10⁻³)²] s = 1 s. Λ isn't a universal constant, though. It depends on the object's size and on the radiation, and for this kind of long-wavelength scattering it falls off steeply as the object shrinks. For a large molecule about 10 nanometres across (a = 10⁻⁶ cm), the estimate is Λ ≈ 10⁻¹² cm⁻² s⁻¹, so with Δx = 10⁻⁶ cm, τ_dec ≈ 1/[10⁻¹² × (10⁻⁶)²] s = 10²⁴ s, far longer than the age of the universe, about 4 × 10¹⁷ s. But a molecule like that is far too light to shift any clock's timing appreciably. The contrast illustrates the difficulty. Larger objects can produce more substantial gravitational effects, but they also tend to become entangled with their environments more readily. Smaller systems may preserve spatial coherence much longer, but their gravitational effects are correspondingly weaker. These particular estimates don’t establish a universal limit, and they don’t rule out more sophisticated gravitational quantum experiments or naturally occurring coherent processes. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, valid when Δx is much smaller than the radiation's wavelength (about a millimetre for the microwave background), which holds in both cases. There’s also research showing that decoherence isn’t confined to ordinary laboratory environments. Danielson, Satishchandran and Wald investigated decoherence associated with Killing horizons, including effects relevant to black holes and certain other spacetime settings. These results show that maintaining quantum coherence in gravitational settings can be difficult for reasons extending beyond familiar laboratory noise. But none of this proves that spontaneous indefinite causal order cannot occur in nature. It would be a mistake to argue that because the laboratory arrangements are delicate, the universe must never produce anything analogous. Nature is not limited to the apparatuses we know how to construct. At the same time, one cannot infer natural ICO simply because quantum gravity might permit it. As far as established observations go, we have laboratory evidence for operational indefinite causal order, but no confirmed observation of its spontaneous occurrence in nature, whether since the Solar System formed or earlier. The deeper possibility involves quantum superpositions of spacetime geometry. Suppose the gravitational field can occupy a coherent superposition of configurations represented schematically by |Ψ⟩ = α|g₁⟩ + β|g₂⟩. If the two geometries produce different causal relationships between relevant interventions, the combined process might not admit a single definite causal order of those interventions. The proposal sometimes called a gravitational quantum switch investigates this possibility. Zych, Costa, Pikovski and Brukner studied a gravitational approach to testing temporal order, and Castro-Ruiz and colleagues examined quantum clocks and the temporal localization of events in the presence of gravitating quantum systems. The idea is that the gravitational field itself could participate in coherently controlling which operation precedes another. Unlike an ordinary photonic switch built in a fixed classical spacetime, such a proposal raises the possibility that the relevant causal structure is associated with quantum geometry rather than merely the paths of a target system through a fixed apparatus. But the gravitational quantum switch remains a proposal, not an experimentally established demonstration of fundamentally indefinite spacetime causal order. Even a successful future experiment would need careful interpretation. One would have to establish what class of definite-order descriptions its results actually exclude. And the mathematical expression α|g₁⟩ + β|g₂⟩ is only schematic. In a full quantum-gravity theory, identifying physical geometries, defining observables, and comparing events across different geometrical configurations are themselves nontrivial problems. This brings us back to the question about time itself. One possibility is that indefinite operational causal order reflects something genuinely fundamental. Perhaps there is no deeper fact specifying which of two operations came first, because the physical theory simply does not contain such a fact. If that turned out to be correct at the level of fundamental spacetime structure, it would challenge the idea that every physical event belongs to one universally definite causal ordering. But it wouldn’t necessarily mean that time doesn’t exist, that ordinary causality is an illusion, or that every event in the universe lacks a definite temporal relationship. A theory could have indefinite order in some circumstances while retaining well-defined causal relations in others. Another possibility is that operationally indefinite order emerges from a deeper physical description with definite ordering. That ordering might be expressed through finer-grained spacetime events, additional physical variables, or some structure not directly represented by the operational process. Such a theory would have to reproduce the observed interference and causal-correlation statistics. It couldn’t simply replace the quantum switch with an ordinary random choice between A-before-B and B-before-A. A third possibility is that our question about which operation “really happened first” isn’t well defined independently of the physical description being used. In that case, the problem may not be that a fact is hidden, but that we’re trying to assign a single ordering relation to objects that don’t possess one in the relevant theory. There is also an important distinction between temporal order and causal order. Special relativity already tells us that the chronological order of spacelike-separated events can depend on the reference frame, while their causal relationship remains constrained by the light-cone structure. Quantum indefinite causal order is not simply another name for relativity of simultaneity. And none of these results, by themselves, settles the philosophical debate between presentism and eternalism. A definite causal structure does not establish that only the present exists, just as an indefinite operational causal structure does not establish that past, present and future all exist equally. One could also consider a generative view of time in which physical reality has one realized history, produced through successive stages, while certain quantum operations lack a definite ordering at the operational level. That is a possible interpretive program, not an experimentally established conclusion. It would need to specify the underlying dynamics and show that they reproduce the relevant quantum predictions. This is close to the distinction between the physical state of a system and the truth of propositions about its history. Suppose a particular event happened but left no recoverable record. The fact that nobody could reconstruct it would not logically imply that it never happened or that there was no fact about it. Conversely, the mere logical possibility of such a hidden fact does not give us evidence that it exists. Those two points are easy to confuse when discussing quantum mechanics. The experiments tell us something definite about which operational descriptions fail. They do not automatically provide a complete ontology of time. So my answer would be that the two alternatives in the question are not necessarily exhaustive, and they aren’t always mutually exclusive. For the effective operations in a quantum switch, the process can be causally nonseparable. An ordinary classical mixture of definite operation orders is not an adequate explanation of its statistics. For the finer-grained interactions in the laboratory, a definite spacetime causal ordering can still exist. For a proposed deeper ordering of reality, the answer depends on the theory. Some possible underlying descriptions may preserve definite facts about trajectories or events; others may not. Whether those descriptions make distinct empirical predictions is a separate question. And for genuinely quantum spacetime, we don’t yet have the experimental evidence needed to decide whether fundamental causal order must be indefinite. What I wouldn’t conclude is that the absence of an operationally definite order proves there is no objective fact about any deeper ordering. Nor would I conclude that a definite underlying order must exist simply because ordinary experience presents events as happening successively. The strongest conclusion currently justified is narrower but still remarkable: quantum theory permits experimentally supported processes whose effective causal ordering cannot be reduced to an ordinary classical choice between two definite orders, even though known laboratory realizations can be understood within a finer-grained, definitely ordered spacetime description. Whether nature ever produces fundamentally indefinite spacetime causal order, and what that would mean for the ontology of time, remains open. I think the important distinction is between what relativity establishes about simultaneous events, whether the universe admits a geometrically distinguished global time, and whether past, present, and future all physically exist. Those are different questions, and answering the first doesn’t automatically settle the others. I’m interpreting the question as referring to the relativity of simultaneity: observers in relative motion can disagree about which spatially separated events occur at the same time. In special relativity, the Lorentz transformation gives: Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c²) where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). If two spatially separated events are simultaneous in one inertial frame, Δt = 0, then another inertial frame moving relative to it generally assigns them different times: Δt′ = −γvΔx/c² That is an experimentally supported feature of relativistic spacetime. But it doesn’t mean simultaneity is meaningless, nor does it allow observers to disagree about the causal order of timelike- or lightlike-related events. It means distant simultaneity depends on the reference frame. The argument that this favors the block universe is more interesting than simply saying that different observers disagree about “now.” In the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, associated with Rietdijk (1966), Putnam (1967), and Penrose’s Andromeda example, the concern is that if every event simultaneous with me is equally real, and every inertial observer’s simultaneity relation has equal authority in determining what exists, then the differing simultaneity relations can be used to argue that events ordinarily classified as past or future must also be real. Imagine two people walking past one another. Their relative motion gives them slightly different planes of distant simultaneity. At the distance of the Andromeda galaxy, even a small relative velocity can produce a substantial difference between which distant events each observer assigns to the same time as their encounter. The disagreement is physical in the sense that the coordinate assignments follow from the Lorentz transformations. But the further claim that everything simultaneous with an observer must exist in the same ontological sense is not itself a Lorentz transformation or an experimentally established law. The argument for eternalism therefore requires additional assumptions connecting simultaneity with existence. Those assumptions deserve consideration, but they aren’t automatically established by relativity. There are several alternatives, although they don’t all preserve the same notion of a global present. Howard Stein’s work on becoming in Minkowski spacetime offers one approach. Instead of requiring a universal simultaneity plane, becoming can be understood relative to individual spacetime events and their causal pasts. What has already become relative to an event is associated with its past light cone, including the event itself. This avoids requiring a global present shared by spatially separated events, although it also gives up the ordinary idea of a universal “now.” Growing-block theories take another approach. They hold that past and present events exist while future events do not yet exist. C. D. Broad developed an influential version, while later proposals, including work by George Ellis and Tony Rothman, explore forms of relativistic or crystallizing becoming. Such approaches still need to explain how the boundary between what exists and what has not yet become is defined consistently with relativity. Causal-set theory offers a different possibility. In the classical sequential-growth models developed by Rideout and Sorkin, a causal set grows by acquiring new elements. The auxiliary order in which causally unrelated elements are introduced has no independent physical significance; the causal structure is what matters. This provides a mathematical model of growth without requiring a physically preferred simultaneity slicing of continuous spacetime. It remains a research framework rather than an experimentally established account of fundamental spacetime. Neo-Lorentzian approaches instead allow an underlying preferred frame or foliation while preserving the observable predictions of special relativity. Such a structure is not required by the established experiments, but the relativity of simultaneity alone doesn’t logically exclude every interpretation containing an empirically hidden preferred frame. Via math alone, can mathematical theories have the math that reveal each event's every specific of the past, present, and future, or are such theories' math only able to reveal "constraints on possible existence" but with Laplace's-demon limitations? Via math alone, meaning a theory's equations and laws without any empirical input, no, and that holds for past, present, and future events alike. A physical theory's math narrows down which events could possibly be the case at any time. But none of our current established theories determines every specific event by its laws alone, since each needs initial conditions and parameters that its laws don't supply. Something other than the laws has to supply those. In some theories, even that doesn't narrow things to a single possibility per time, whether that time is past, present, or future. The simplest way to see this is Newton's second law, F = ma. Take a free particle, so F = 0. Then a = 0, and integrating twice gives x(t) = x₀ + v₀t. The equation has done real work: it rules out the particle speeding up, slowing down, or curving. But x₀ and v₀ are left completely open. Every choice of them gives a different trajectory, and every one of those trajectories satisfies the law equally well. So at any given t, whether it's an earlier time, the present, or a later one, the law alone leaves infinitely many candidate positions. Notice that this includes the present: the law doesn't tell you where the particle is now any more than where it was or will be. A spring works the same way. From m d²x/dt² = −kx you get x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), with ω = √(k/m). The math fixes the frequency, which is a genuine constraint. The amplitude A and phase φ, though, are only pinned down once you specify the spring's position and velocity at some time, and nothing in the equation itself supplies those. Scale that up and nothing changes in principle. Einstein's field equations, G_μν = (8πG/c⁴)T_μν, have an enormous space of solutions: black holes, expanding universes, gravitational waves, and plenty of exotic spacetimes nobody thinks are physically realistic. General relativity actually makes the narrowing unusually explicit. The data you specify on a slice of spacetime, including the slice you'd call the present, has to satisfy the Hamiltonian and momentum constraint equations. Not every configuration is admissible, but infinitely many still are. On top of that, dimensionless constants like the fine-structure constant (about 1/137 at low energies) are measured, not derived. The Standard Model alone has 19 such free parameters in its basic form, and around 26 once neutrino masses are included. Now set up a complete initial-value problem: the equations plus fully specified data at one time, say the present. A deterministic theory with well-behaved equations then returns exactly one solution, and it fixes things in both directions. Plug measured values of x₀ and v₀ into x(t) = x₀ + v₀t and you get one position for every t, earlier as well as later. That's the Laplace's demon idea: an intellect for whom both past and future would be calculable from the present state. It's perfectly good mathematics, but notice what it leaves out. The present state is the input. The demon doesn't get the specifics of the present from the math at all. It gets them from the data, and only then does the math fix the specifics at other times. Even with complete data, uniqueness isn't guaranteed. Norton's dome is the standard example. A ball rests at the apex of a dome shaped so that, in suitable units, its equation of motion is d²r/dt² = √r. One solution has the ball sit there forever, r(t) = 0. But for any later time T you like, there is also a solution where the ball sits at r = 0 until T and then follows r(t) = (t − T)⁴/144. You can check it. For t ≥ T the second derivative is (t − T)²/12, which is exactly √r, and position and velocity both match up smoothly at T. The equation is also unchanged when you run time backward, so the mirror-image solutions work too. A ball resting at the apex now is equally consistent with having sat there forever, or with having rolled up and come to rest there at any earlier time T, following r(t) = (T − t)⁴/144 until it arrives. Same law, same present data, infinitely many possible futures and infinitely many possible pasts. The dome is a special construction, and people still argue about how much it tells us about realistic Newtonian systems. Mathematically, though, it's a clean counterexample to guaranteed uniqueness. General relativity has an analogous issue in certain spacetimes with Cauchy horizons, beyond which the data on a slice no longer fixes a unique continuation. That's a feature of particular solutions, not of the theory across the board. Determinism also isn't the same as predictability. In chaotic systems a small error δ₀ in your data typically grows roughly like δ₀e^(λt) over a range of times, where λ is the Lyapunov exponent. So "fixed in principle" doesn't mean "knowable in practice." Running the equations backward is no better: in typical chaotic systems, errors grow just as quickly when you retrodict as when you predict. Quantum mechanics adds another layer. The Schrödinger equation, iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, is deterministic for ψ in both directions: given ψ at one time, it's fixed at every earlier and later time. But what ψ tells you about actual measurement outcomes is probabilities, via the Born rule P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|². So in its standard formulation, even complete present data narrows a future measurement's outcome only to a weighted set of candidates. Retrodiction has a parallel problem. On readings where measurement collapses ψ, many different earlier states could have led to the same post-measurement state, so present data doesn't single out earlier specifics either. Whether the underlying physics is genuinely indeterministic depends on interpretation. Bohmian mechanics adds definite particle positions plus a guidance equation that steers them. That gets you single outcomes, but only once you feed in exact particle positions as further data. Many-worlds keeps only the Schrödinger equation and says every outcome occurs on some branch, though how probability fits into that picture is itself debated. Collapse theories such as GRW go the other way, building genuine randomness into the dynamics itself. The math alone doesn't decide between these. Could a theory ever fix every specific, past, present, and future, by its laws alone? Only if its equations admitted exactly one solution, with no free data and no free constants. Even then, for a quantum theory a unique state fixes probabilities for outcomes, so whether that counts as fixing every specific event depends on interpretation. Some proposals push in this direction. Hartle and Hawking's no-boundary proposal, for instance, tries to make the universe's initial quantum state a matter of law rather than input. Even so, what it aims to yield is probabilities for different possible courses of cosmic events, not one detailed course of events, and it isn't established. More generally, even a theory with a single solution would still need observation to confirm it describes our world rather than just a consistent mathematical one, because a uniqueness proof says nothing about which model reality matches. And bare math doesn't even say what its symbols stand for. It takes rules linking them to observables to turn equations into a physical theory at all. There's also the "per given time" aspect of the question. In all of these equations, t is just a parameter. Nothing in x(t) = x₀ + v₀t marks any particular value of t as now. That's true even in Newtonian physics with absolute time: absolute time gives you a single ordering of instants, but the equations don't say which instant is present. Einstein reportedly told Carnap that this bothered him, that physics has no place for the Now. So the math can't, on its own, even sort events into past, present, and future. That sorting has to come from outside the equations. Whether only the present exists is a question the equations stay silent on either way. So the second description is the accurate one, for past, present, and future alike, as long as "math alone" means the laws without empirical input. The laws reveal constraints on possible existence, sometimes drastic ones. But no current established theory's laws supply the conditions and parameters needed to single out our universe's actual course of events. On their own, they leave many possibilities open at every time, including the present. Supply complete data at one time, and a deterministic theory with well-behaved equations can narrow things to one in both directions, subject to Laplace's demon's limitations. Even then, the specifics at the time you supplied data for come from the data, not the math. Uniqueness can fail toward the future or the past, chaos limits prediction and retrodiction alike, and a unique mathematical solution still has to be checked against reality. In indeterministic theories, even complete data leaves a spread of possibilities. In short, the laws constrain what can be the case at any time, past, present, or future, often severely, but they don't by themselves determine which events were, are, or will be the case. Is "numerical identity" via "'measure of time' plus 'persistence conditions'"? Yes, as long as "measure of time" means what it should mean, which is time's order and its magnitude, and not the act of reading a clock. Numerical identity across times comes from the order of time plus the persistence conditions of whatever kind of thing you're talking about. Magnitude comes in only when those conditions say something about duration. Clock readings and records are how we find out about identity, not what makes it the case. Each piece of that needs unpacking, so here's the longer version. Numerical identity means being one and the same thing, as opposed to qualitative identity, which means being exactly alike. Two freshly minted coins can be qualitatively identical and still be two coins. At a single moment numerical identity is trivial, since everything is identical to itself and time doesn't come into it. The interesting question is identity across times: what makes the thing here now the same thing as the one here last year. That's the question of persistence, and that's where time comes in. By "the measure of time" I mean two things that are easy to run together. The first is order: which stage comes before which. The second is magnitude: how much time separates them. Aristotle had both in his definition, time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after," while Leibniz's leaner version, time as "the order of successive things," keeps only the first. You can see they're different because you can change one without the other. Take any labeling of moments by τ and relabel it with σ = F(τ), where F is any function with F′ > 0. Every before and after stays exactly where it was, so the order is untouched, but every duration has been distorted. Order alone never tells you how long anything took. Precision is a third thing again. "The 21st century" and "2/4/2010" differ in how finely they locate something, but both already rely on magnitude, since a century and a day are units of duration. And the measure of time isn't the same as measuring time. Measuring is something a clock does, and it presupposes an order and a magnitude for it to measure. Weighing a rock tells you its mass, but the rock had that mass before anyone weighed it. It's also fair to ask what makes an order temporal at all, since points along a ruler are ordered too. Being able to tell one event from another isn't enough, because two distinct events can happen at the same time. What makes an order temporal is succession: its members aren't co-present, each comes after the one before, and on the view that only the present exists, the earlier stage no longer exists once the later one does. Time also isn't the same as the state of things at a time, which is why it makes sense to ask "at what time did that state occur?" The same kind of state can occur at two different times, and a periodic clock shows the same reading at different times, so the time can't simply be the state. Now, cosmological time. In relativity every clock measures its own proper time, the time elapsed along its own path through spacetime, its worldline. For a path γ, that's L(γ) = (1/c) ∫ √(−g_μν dx^μ dx^ν) where g_μν is the metric (sign convention −,+,+,+). Two clocks that start together and later meet again can disagree about how much time went by. That's the twin effect, and when the twins compare readings side by side, nobody in any frame disputes what the clocks say. Proper time is local, though. Cosmological time is built from it but is global. At any event p it's defined as τ(p) = sup { L(γ) : γ a past-directed causal curve from p } which in words is the longest proper time that could have elapsed along any causal path reaching p from its past; if the past has a beginning, that's the longest time since the beginning. Andersson, Galloway and Howard worked this out in 1998. When τ is finite everywhere and goes to zero along every causal curve traced back as far as it goes, they call it regular. Regularity is what makes it well behaved. It forces the spacetime to be globally hyperbolic, which among other things rules out closed timelike curves, so "earlier" and "later" can never loop back on themselves. And the surfaces of constant τ slice spacetime into whole-universe "nows": every worldline that continues past one crosses it exactly once, and every causal path traced back from a later event runs through it, so nothing off the slice can influence what comes after it. (Technically they're future Cauchy surfaces, not always full Cauchy surfaces, because a worldline that ends early, say at a black hole's singularity, never reaches the later slices.) So cosmological time isn't a second kind of time competing with proper time. It is proper time, taken at its maximum. That definition does depend on the past being finite in the right way. If the past goes on forever in proper time, so that causal curves reaching p can have unboundedly long proper times, then τ(p) = +∞ and if the universe is uniform on large scales, that's true at every point, so τ has no finite value anywhere. The flat, empty spacetime of special relativity is the textbook case. That isn't a flaw in the idea, just a fact about which spacetimes have a cosmological time. Whether ours does is open. The standard cosmological model, which the CMB supports, gives a finite, regular τ. It does so only by extrapolating back to a(t) → 0, beyond the epochs observations test, and some live proposals for the earliest history, such as a bounce or a long or endless phase before the hot Big Bang, would make τ infinite. So nothing below assumes our universe's past is finite. Here's the condition once, for everything that follows: what I say about τ in general holds in any spacetime where τ is regular, and what I say about τ in our universe holds on the standard model. Whether time has an end doesn't matter, since τ only looks backward. And nothing in the account of identity below depends on time having a beginning or an end. Order and magnitude, and particular times and intervals, make sense either way. What a regular τ provides is one common standard for them, set by the geometry itself. In a hypothetical universe with an endless past, you'd have to measure from some physically distinguished slice instead, such as the moment of a bounce, taking the longest proper time from that slice rather than from a beginning. That standard would be only as good as the slice is distinguished. For our universe this fallback is close at hand. The standard model assigns only minutes to everything before the earliest epoch we test directly, the formation of the first light nuclei. So the figures below, such as the 13.8 billion years, are, to within those minutes, the longest proper time since that epoch, whatever came before it. In an idealized expanding universe with the FLRW metric ds² = −c² dt² + a(t)² dΣ² that begins in a Big Bang, with a(t) → 0 as t → 0, the cosmological time is just t, the proper time of observers who ride along with the expansion, the comoving observers. That's what cosmologists mean by the age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years. (Without such a beginning the same metric can give τ = ∞, as de Sitter expansion, a(t) ∝ e^(Ht), does when its t runs back without limit.) In our actual, lumpier universe, τ's slices still exist; only their match with comoving clocks is approximate. A clock that moves relative to the comoving observers accumulates less time. In FLRW, a clock with peculiar velocity v, measured by the local comoving observers, ticks at dτ = dt √(1 − v²/c²) and in a weak gravitational field you get approximately dτ ≈ dt (1 + Φ/c² − v²/2c²) where Φ is the gravitational potential, negative inside a well. At present our motion relative to the cosmic background, about 370 km/s, costs our clocks roughly v²/2c² ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, about 24 seconds a year, and the gravitational wells we sit in add more. More generally, if Sₐ is the surface where τ = a, then for any event q with τ(q) ≥ a, τ(q) = a + d(Sₐ, q) where d(Sₐ, q) is the longest proper time along any causal curve from Sₐ to q. So between cosmic times a and b, no clock anywhere can accumulate more than b − a. Measured against cosmological time, local clocks run at or below it and never above. When you compare two local clocks with each other, one can look stretched relative to the other, but against the common time they all sit at or under the same ceiling. Here's a way to picture it. In a universe with a Big Bang, imagine indestructible clocks started there and carried along every possible path, so there's one at every location and none ever stops. When you look at them, they disagree, because they've moved differently and sat in different gravitational wells. Now pick any event, look at all the clocks that could have gotten there, and take the one showing the most elapsed time. That reading is τ at that event. And that top reading is actually achieved, not just approached: when cosmological time is regular, a theorem guarantees a freely falling path from the initial singularity to every event that realizes the maximum. The clocks that come out on top are the ones that never fought the expansion and just rode along with it: the comoving ones. What makes their time common is that, in a homogeneous universe, all the comoving clocks read the same at the same cosmic moment everywhere, without anyone ever synchronizing them. They agree because they all started at the same beginning and the universe has been the same everywhere on average ever since. Every other clock reads less than that common value. So the "oldest clock" reading isn't any one clock's private time. It's the ceiling all of them share, and every local clock's shortfall is measured against it. This isn't just a thought experiment, because the universe gives us something very close to that common clock: the cosmic microwave background. It's the light left over from when the universe became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, and it fills every location. It lines up with cosmological time in three ways. First, it's the same in every direction to about one part in 100,000 once you take out the effect of our own motion, and its spectrum is the most perfect blackbody ever measured in nature. COBE's FIRAS instrument found deviations below about 50 parts per million of the peak, with a temperature today of T₀ = 2.72548 ± 0.00057 K. A blackbody that clean means the radiation was once in thermal equilibrium throughout the region we can see, which is the common hot early phase that the comoving clocks share. Uniformity that good is also what makes the idealized FLRW picture a good approximation to our universe, and that picture is where cosmological time coincides with the proper time of comoving clocks. Second, the temperature works as a clock. As the universe expands, the radiation cools as T ∝ 1/a(t), so at redshift z it should be T(z) = T₀ (1 + z) That has been checked independently. Molecules in a galaxy at z = 0.89, excited by the background radiation, give 5.08 ± 0.10 K against a predicted 5.15 K. The Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect in 158 galaxy clusters out to z ≈ 1.35 constrains any departure of the form T(z) = T₀(1 + z)^(1−α) to α = 0.011 ± 0.016, consistent with zero. So, as far as the universe is uniform on large scales, which we can check only within our observable region, comoving observers who measure the same background temperature are at the same cosmic time, counted from the hot early phase, with no synchronization convention involved. Fitting the full CMB data within the standard model gives the cosmological time at our location, the age of the universe, as 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years. The oldest stars and globular clusters come out at roughly 12 to 13 billion years, younger than that ceiling, which is what you'd expect if no clock can have run longer than cosmological time. Third, the CMB picks out the frame. On top of the uniform glow there's a dipole of about 3.36 mK, hotter in one direction and colder in the opposite one. That's the Doppler signature of our own motion at about 370 km/s relative to the comoving frame. Planck even detected the aberration and Doppler boosting of the small-scale pattern that this motion should produce, which confirms that the dipole is mostly our motion and not something built into the radiation. The frame in which the dipole vanishes is the frame of the comoving clocks, whose proper time is cosmological time. "Perfectly" would be too strong, though, and the reasons are worth knowing. The one-part-in-100,000 ripples are real. They're the seeds of galaxies, and they're why the match between cosmological time and comoving clocks holds only approximately in our actual, lumpy universe. There's also an open puzzle. Counts of distant quasars and radio galaxies show a dipole pointing roughly the same way as the CMB's, though how closely depends on the sample, but about two to two and a half times larger than our motion should produce, at around five sigma. A 2026 analysis of about 637,000 active galaxies still finds it. Its authors suspect local structure and selection effects, and consider a genuine mismatch between the rest frames of matter and radiation unlikely. Even if such a mismatch held up, it would complicate the picture without removing cosmological time. τ is defined by the spacetime geometry, and the CMB is the strongest evidence we have that the geometry is close to the uniform kind in which τ is the comoving clocks' time. None of this proves the τ-slices are "the present." What it does show is that the common frame, and a common time counted from the hot Big Bang epoch, are physical features of our universe, not conventions anyone chose. That this common time is τ itself, counted from a beginning, holds on the standard model. This is where Einstein's relativity of simultaneity comes in, and I want to be careful, because the relativity of simultaneity isn't wrong about what it actually says. In 1905 Einstein pointed out that we have no common time for two distant places A and B until we define one, and he defined it by stipulating that light takes as long to go from A to B as from B to A. Synchronize clocks that way in one inertial frame, do the same in a frame moving at speed v relative to the first, and the two sets of clocks disagree about which distant events happen at the same time. The Lorentz transformation shows it directly: t′ = γ (t − v x / c²), γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) so two events with Δt = 0 but separated by Δx get Δt′ = −γ v Δx / c², which isn't zero. That's correct, and no experiment done inside a sealed lab will tell you which frame's synchronization is the right one, because the laws are the same in all of them. The misconception is taking that synchronization procedure, which is local clock time extended across space by a convention, as if it were what "at the same time" means. Take that as the definition, and the relativity of simultaneity turns into the conclusion that there's no physical fact at all about which distant events are happening at the same time. Einstein himself leaned that way late in life, writing that the distinction between past, present and future is "only a stubbornly persistent illusion." But that conclusion follows only if light-signal synchronization within some inertial frame is the only thing "at the same time" could mean. The spacetime of special relativity is empty and perfectly symmetric, so naturally nothing in it singles out one frame; it doesn't even have a finite cosmological time. Our universe isn't empty. It has matter and radiation with a rest frame of their own, and we measure our velocity relative to that frame from the dipole in the microwave background. On the standard model, its geometry also has a regular cosmological time, and that gives a sense of "at the same time" that uses no signaling convention at all: the comoving clocks are in step because of their common beginning and the universe's large-scale uniformity, not because anyone exchanged light pulses. The τ function and its slices are facts about the geometry. What's proposed is reading "same τ" as "at the same time." On that proposal, two events are co-present when they have the same τ, and that doesn't depend on any observer's choice of frame. The laws stay exactly as Lorentz invariant as before. What picks out the cosmic frame is the actual contents and shape of the universe, not the laws, which is why the sealed-lab argument never touches it. To be fair about the limits, relativity guarantees that a spacetime like ours can be sliced into whole-universe moments, but in many ways, and it doesn't pick one. Cosmological time picks one from the geometry alone, unique up to relabeling and independent of any coordinates or observers, which makes it geometrically distinguished. Treating it as the real "now" goes a step further: relativity permits that but doesn't force it, and τ isn't the only convention-free candidate. In many cosmological spacetimes the geometry also fixes a slicing by surfaces of constant mean curvature. That slicing agrees with τ in idealized FLRW but generally differs in a lumpy universe. What singles τ out is that it's the ceiling on every clock's elapsed proper time, so preferring it is part of the philosophical commitment. What relativity gives with no commitment is that wherever τ is regular, it exists, is fixed by the geometry, and bounds every clock's elapsed time. A drifting clock is a separate problem, and it needs less. A real clock, especially a quantum one, can misbehave. Picture a quantum clock whose pointer hops around a ring of positions, advancing on average but with some probability of hopping backward. Record its readings at two successive events and the later event can carry the earlier reading. If identity ran through clock readings, a bad clock could change what persisted, which is absurd. What the drifting clock gets wrong first is the causal order. Along one worldline, which of two events is later is fixed by the light-cone structure, in any spacetime with a consistent future direction and no closed timelike curves, whether or not τ is regular. And τ agrees with that order on every worldline at once, because it can't run backward along any of them. If q lies to the future of p along a timelike curve of length ℓ, then any path from the beginning to p, extended by that curve, is a path from the beginning to q that's longer by ℓ, so τ(q) ≥ τ(p) + ℓ > τ(p) It also increases along light-like paths. So τ goes up along every future-directed causal path, and the later event really has the larger τ, whatever the clock said. The commonality does its real work beyond a single worldline: it compares durations across different worldlines and says which distant events are co-present. τ isn't one more clock that might also be wrong. It's the common time every clock is measured against, and its authority comes from the geometry, not from any one device. The twins' clocks can disagree by years while the order of what happened to each twin is never in doubt, because that order is causal. One caveat: τ settles the fact, not our knowledge of it. We get at τ only through measurement (comoving clocks, the background temperature, cosmological models), and "13.8 billion years" has error bars. And in quantum settings where the clocks themselves are in superposition, how to recover a definite order of events from clock data alone is still an open problem. The fact is definite, but our access to it goes through the same kind of measurement it judges. So, back to identity. Persisting means being the same thing across successive stages, and that can't even be stated without an order. Causal inheritance has a direction: "B came from A" and "A came from B" involve the same two states and differ only in order. Fission, one thing becoming two, and fusion, two things becoming one, can involve the very same states in opposite orders. "Uninterrupted" is a claim about the structure of the order, and a thing's end is its last stage, which needs an order to be last in. So the order is necessary. But it isn't sufficient. A perfect duplicate sits in exactly the same order as the original. Or take a uranium-238 nucleus that decays at some random moment. The order is fixed by what actually happened: it decayed then and not before. But what persisted? The U-238 nucleus has come to its end, its nucleons carry on, and a thorium nucleus and an alpha particle begin. One order gives three different answers, so something other than the order has to be deciding them. That something is the persistence conditions of each kind of thing: what a thing of that kind has to satisfy to stay itself across successive stages. They differ by kind. An organism, a hurricane, a nucleus and a field all persist under different conditions, and something that can't end has no termination condition at all. This is also why a state isn't the same as the thing it's a state of. The state at a given time ceases even if a qualitatively identical state follows it, while the thing whose state it was carries on as long as its conditions are met, unless it has undergone its end. Chance doesn't change any of this. Even when the next state is random, it still arises from the state before it under laws that set the probabilities. The order is fixed by what occurred, but the order alone still doesn't tell you what continued. For people, the persistence conditions are genuinely open. Psychological continuity alone can't be it. Suppose one person split into two successors with the same memories and personality, and continuity guaranteed identity. Then both successors would be the original. Since identity is transitive (A = B and A = C gives B = C), they'd also be the same person as each other, which they plainly aren't. That's Parfit's fission problem. Causal ancestry alone can't be it either, since a child is causally descended from a parent without being the parent. A plausible candidate is uninterrupted causal inheritance of an embodied organization, as opposed to reconstruction from a record of it. But that's a proposal, not a result, and the answer may be partly a matter of which criterion we choose rather than a fact waiting to be discovered. Magnitude matters only where a kind's conditions mention duration. A hurricane or a person doesn't need to last a certain number of hours to stay itself; a 24-hour ceasefire does. And the relabeling point from earlier shows that identity doesn't depend on the numbers on the labels at all. Replace τ with any σ = F(τ) where F′ > 0 and every identity verdict stays the same. The order is untouched, and so are physical durations like proper time, which are the ones a duration-sensitive kind's conditions refer to. As for the act of measuring, it's neither necessary nor sufficient. Things persist whether or not anyone measures anything. Measuring presupposes identity rather than producing it: a reading of 9:00 and a later reading of 10:00 measure an hour only if they came from the same clock. Swap the clock for an identical-looking replacement in between and you have two readings from two clocks, not an hour. Measurement can also get the order wrong, as the drifting clock shows. Measurement belongs on the evidence side. Records can establish which of two successors went through which procedure, but deciding which one, if either, is the original still takes a theory of persistence. And every measurement happens in the present: comparisons with earlier times go through present records, and comparisons with later times go through the present state plus the laws. Finally, a block universe doesn't get out of any of this. Cosmological time is defined by the geometry of spacetime, and someone who holds that past, present and future all exist accepts that same spacetime. On the standard model, that means the same τ with the same values, the same ceiling on every clock, the same age of the universe, and the same rest frame in the microwave background. A drifting clock in a block universe gets the order wrong for exactly the same reason. The only difference is what the τ-slices are taken to be. In the block, every slice exists, so the present isn't the only existence of all duration. The order is simply there rather than something that happens, and no slice is singled out as now. Someone holding that view can refuse to treat any τ-slice as the present, but they can't refuse τ itself, any more than they can refuse the age of the universe. If they set it aside, "the state of the universe at a time" stays relative to however they choose to slice spacetime. The account of identity carries over unchanged: order plus persistence conditions. Having every stage exist doesn't settle which stages are stages of one thing. The persistence conditions still decide that. So, to the question: yes. Numerical identity across times is via the measure of time plus the persistence conditions of the kind of thing in question. The measure of time here means time's order and, where a kind's conditions require it, time's magnitude, both fixed by cosmological time as one common, definite standard in any spacetime where it's regular, the standard model of ours included. Where it isn't, the order along each worldline is still fixed by the causal structure and durations along it by proper time, but a single common standard needs a physically distinguished slice to count from. The order is necessary but not sufficient, and the persistence conditions supply what the order can't, though they only apply across an order. The act of measuring time is neither necessary nor sufficient: it presupposes the identity of the clock doing the measuring, it can be wrong, and it answers to the actual order and durations, which cosmological time fixes. Is our universe's cosmological time gapless & does each τ-slice have every part of our universe co-occurring during cosmological time? Regarding the standard cosmological model, yes to both, though each "yes" needs pinning down. Cosmological time is continuous, so its slices form an unbroken succession. No cosmic time is skipped, and no worldline jumps over any slice. Whether time stays continuous at the very smallest scales is a separate and open question. And each τ-slice contains exactly one moment of every part of the universe that exists at that cosmic time, all at the same value of τ, none able to affect another, together making a state of the universe that everything later traces back through. Here's the longer version. In the block picture, spacetime is a loaf. All of it, past, present and future together, is the universe as a whole, and a slice is a three-dimensional cross-section: the whole of space at one instant. The catch relativity adds is that the loaf doesn't come pre-sliced. Observers moving relative to each other cut it at different angles, because their clocks, synchronized by light signals in their own frames, disagree about which distant events are simultaneous: t′ = γ (t − v x / c²), γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) so two events with Δt = 0, a distance Δx apart, get Δt′ = −γ v Δx / c². Nothing in the laws picks one cut over another. What can pick one is the geometry of the particular loaf, and that's where cosmological time comes in. Every clock measures proper time along its own path through spacetime, its worldline. For a path λ, L(λ) = (1/c) ∫ √(−g_μν dx^μ dx^ν) where g_μν is the metric (sign convention −,+,+,+). That's local: it orders and measures events along one worldline and says nothing about anything off it. Andersson, Galloway and Howard (1998) built a global time out of it. At any event p, τ(p) = sup { L(λ) : λ a past-directed causal curve from p } which is the longest proper time that could have elapsed along any causal path reaching p from its past. When τ is finite everywhere and goes to zero along every causal curve traced back as far as it goes, they call it regular, and they proved several things about it. It's a time function, meaning it strictly increases along every future-directed causal curve: p causally before q ⟹ τ(p) < τ(q) Its level sets, the surfaces τ = constant, are future Cauchy surfaces: every worldline that continues past one crosses it exactly once, and every causal path traced back from a later event runs through it. (They're future Cauchy surfaces rather than full ones: a worldline that ends early, say at a black hole's singularity, never reaches the later slices.) And it's fixed by the geometry alone: once you adopt this definition, no coordinates or observers enter, and its values are settled. You could relabel the slices with any strictly increasing σ = F(τ), which changes the numbers but not which events share a slice. So a τ-slice is a cut of the loaf chosen by the loaf's own shape, spatially whole and temporally an instant. In that sense it really is an instant of the universe as a whole. A common objection is that spacetime has no intrinsic time at all, that time and space only appear once a coordinate system is chosen, and that the metric itself presupposes one. That runs together the metric and its components. The metric is a tensor field g: at each event it takes two directions and returns a number, with no coordinates involved, and g_μν dx^μ dx^ν is just that object written out in one particular chart. Change charts and the components change, g′_μν = (∂x^α/∂x′^μ)(∂x^β/∂x′^ν) g_αβ in exactly the way that leaves the interval unchanged. A flat sheet of paper shows the same thing. In Cartesian coordinates ds² = dx² + dy², and in polar coordinates ds² = dr² + r² dθ². The components are different, but the distance between any two marked points comes out the same either way. You need some chart to compute a number, the way you need some ruler, but which one you pick doesn't change the answer. Nature doesn't need a chart at all: a clock carried along a worldline measures its proper time without consulting any coordinates, which is why the twins' age difference is a plain fact. Nor is the metric neutral between time and space. At every event it sorts directions into three classes: timelike if g(v, v) < 0, null if g(v, v) = 0, and spacelike if g(v, v) > 0. That's the light cone, and no change of coordinates or orientation ever moves a direction from one class to another. It isn't a measurement bias either. The signature is what makes the field equations hyperbolic, which is what lets anything propagate. The wave equation −(1/c²) ∂²ψ/∂t² + ∇²ψ = 0 carries signals at speed c. Flip the sign to make time just another direction, (1/c²) ∂²ψ/∂t² + ∇²ψ = 0 and you get a four-dimensional Laplace equation, which is elliptic: it doesn't describe finite-speed wave propagation at all, and, as Hadamard showed, trying to evolve it from one slice to the next is ill-posed, since arbitrarily small changes in the data produce arbitrarily large changes in the solution. Whether empty space is fully isotropic in four dimensions or has Lorentz symmetry is also an empirical question, because the two make different predictions. Full isotropy would put time and space directions on an equal footing, with no light cone and so no speed limit built into the geometry. What we observe, in every test of special relativity from Michelson–Morley onward, is a universal speed limit and time dilation: rotations and boosts mix directions within each class, never across the cone. It's true that empty, flat spacetime singles out no particular timelike direction, so it has no preferred frame. Separately, its cosmological time is infinite, because causal paths of arbitrarily long proper time reach every event. But even there, time and space are not interchangeable. And the geometry doesn't need any predefined reference. Hawking, King and McCarthy (1976) and Malament (1977) showed that, under mild causality conditions, which events can send signals to which, a purely physical relation, fixes the spacetime geometry up to a local scale factor, and clocks supply the scale. Einstein's hole argument points the same way. Its lesson, on the standard reading from Einstein's own resolution through Earman and Norton's revival of it in 1987, is that bare manifold points have no physical identity apart from the metric and the matter. What's physical is whatever diffeomorphisms, smooth reshufflings of the points, leave unchanged. Diffeomorphisms never change the signature or the light cones, and cosmological time is exactly that kind of invariant, built from the metric alone. If φ is any diffeomorphism and φ*g the reshuffled metric, then τ_{φ*g} = τ_g ∘ φ so the reshuffling just carries τ along with the events, and every relation such as "these two events have the same τ" survives untouched. If you'd rather call the metric content than geometry, as many relationalists do by treating it as the gravitational field, nothing here changes. Our universe has that content, and the distinction between time and space is a feature of it, not of our measurements. What has no time or space at all is a bare manifold with the metric stripped away, and that isn't a vacuum; it just isn't spacetime. In an idealized expanding universe with the FLRW metric ds² = −c² dt² + a(t)² dΣ² beginning in a Big Bang (a(t) → 0 as t → 0), τ is just the cosmic time t, the proper time of observers who ride along with the expansion. That's what "the age of the universe" means, about 13.8 billion years. The cosmic microwave background is the strongest evidence that our universe is close to this picture. It has the same temperature in every direction to about one part in 100,000 once our own motion is removed, and it cools with the expansion as T(z) = T₀ (1 + z) with T₀ ≈ 2.725 K, a relation that has been checked out past redshift 1. So comoving observers anywhere who measure the same background temperature are at the same cosmic time, to good approximation, without anyone ever synchronizing anything. The dipole in that radiation, about 3.36 mK, is our own motion at roughly 370 km/s relative to the comoving frame, the frame whose clocks keep cosmological time to good approximation. Now, gapless. Andersson, Galloway and Howard showed that a regular τ is continuous (in fact locally Lipschitz). A continuous function on a connected spacetime takes all the values in between any two it takes, so the values of τ fill an unbroken interval. Since τ is positive everywhere and goes to zero toward the past without ever reaching it, that interval is 0 < τ < T where T is infinite if the universe expands forever, as the standard model says ours does. So no cosmic time is skipped: between any two slices there's a slice for every value in between. The same holds along every worldline. If a future-directed worldline λ runs from τ = a to τ = b, then τ along it is continuous and strictly increasing, so for every c between a and b there's exactly one point on λ where τ = c. No worldline jumps over a slice. Notice also what the open bottom end means. There's no first slice. The beginning is a boundary of the loaf, not an instant in it, so cosmic time starts without a first moment, the way the numbers greater than zero have no smallest member. Two things can look like gaps but aren't. The first is the shape of the slices. In a lumpy universe τ stops being smooth where the longest-time paths from the past stop being unique, so the slices can pick up creases. Near a black hole, a slice can run up against the singularity, which isn't a place in spacetime but marks where spacetime stops, so the slice is left incomplete there, and a worldline that ends at the singularity simply stops crossing later slices; that's something ending, not time skipping. More generally, regularity alone doesn't guarantee that every slice is smooth or even connected. None of that is a gap in time: gaplessness is about τ's values, which still fill an unbroken interval, and about each worldline, which still passes through every value between any two it reaches. The second is an endless past. If causal paths reaching an event could have unboundedly long proper times, as in some models where the past goes on forever, τ would be infinite there rather than gappy, and you'd count instead from some physically distinguished slice, such as a bounce. The real open question is the smallest scales. Continuity is a feature of spacetime as general relativity describes it, and nobody knows whether that description holds down to the Planck time, t_P = √(ħG/c⁵) ≈ 5.39 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s. Some approaches to quantum gravity, causal sets for instance, make spacetime fundamentally discrete. It's worth separating two senses of "gapless" here. If it means continuous, that's what's open at the Planck scale. If it means that each instant follows the one immediately before it with nothing in between, that could still hold in a discrete world, as long as that world has whole-universe instants at all; there'd just be a smallest step. Some discrete approaches, causal sets among them, don't come with global instants, only a discrete web of before-and-after relations, and there the question would have to be restated. Now, co-occurrence. Read the time-function property backward: two different events with the same τ can't be causally related, so they're spacelike-separated. A τ-slice therefore holds exactly one moment of every worldline that reaches that cosmic time, and none of those moments can affect any other. That includes galaxies beyond our horizons, ones we've never exchanged a signal with and, beyond our event horizon at roughly 16 billion light-years, never will. What makes this work without any signals is that τ(p) is fixed entirely by p's own causal past, since it's a supremum over curves running back from p. τ is one function on the whole spacetime, each event gets its value from its own past, and the slices it defines span regions that have never been in contact. Regularity guarantees that τ runs down to the same limiting value, zero, along every worldline traced back, even though they needn't run back to one common point. And in a universe that's close to uniform on large scales, as the CMB shows ours is, equal τ also marks the same epoch of the large-scale expansion, with the same background temperature to about one part in 100,000, in regions that never exchanged a signal, even though local conditions, a galaxy versus a void, can differ enormously. The simultaneity τ defines doesn't depend on any observer or choice: which events share a τ-slice is fixed by the geometry. The slice is also complete in the causal sense. Every causal path traced back from a later event runs through it, so with well-posed laws, a slice regular enough for the equations in question, and suitable data on it (in general relativity, data that satisfy its constraint equations), those data determine everything later in the loaf, or its probabilities where chance is involved. The data include instantaneous rates (velocities, momenta, how fast fields are changing), so what's under way in each part is part of the slice too. "Something occurring" in every part is meant literally, not just a frozen arrangement. Co-occurring at the same cosmic time doesn't mean having the same local age, though. Along any worldline, local proper time runs at or below cosmic time. In FLRW, for a clock with peculiar velocity v relative to the local comoving observers, dτ_local = dt √(1 − v²/c²) and more generally, if Sₐ is the slice τ = a, then for any event q with τ(q) ≥ a, τ(q) = a + d(Sₐ, q) where d(Sₐ, q) is the longest proper time along any causal curve from Sₐ to q. So between slices a and b, nothing accumulates more than b − a. An astronaut back from a fast trip and her stay-at-home twin are on the same slice, co-occurring, with different ages. Our own motion through the cosmic background costs our clocks roughly v²/2c² ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, about 24 seconds a year relative to comoving clocks on the same slices, before counting gravity and our other motions. In the block, every τ-slice exists equally. The loaf is the universe as a whole, and a τ-slice is one full-width instant of it, so co-occurring just means sharing a slice. None of that needs a special now, and the standard block doesn't have one. It's worth being clear about what the geometry settles. That the τ-slices exist, that their values run unbroken, and that they hold exactly one moment of every part are facts about the loaf, and so is the simultaneity they define. What the geometry doesn't settle is whether τ-slices are privileged over other ways of cutting the loaf. τ isn't the only geometry-defined candidate. In many cosmological spacetimes there's also a slicing by surfaces of constant mean curvature, which agrees with τ in an idealized uniform universe but not in a lumpy one. What singles τ out is that it's the ceiling on every clock's elapsed proper time. But the question here doesn't need τ to be privileged, only that it's there. Finally, whether our universe has a regular τ at all is open. The standard cosmological model gives one, but only by extrapolating back past anything observations test. Some proposals for the earliest epochs, such as a bounce after an endless contraction, could make τ infinite. And part of that openness is permanent: regularity is a property of the whole spacetime, including regions we can never observe, so observations can only ever support it through a model. Even so, the standard model assigns only minutes to everything before the earliest epoch we test directly, the formation of the first light nuclei. Counting from that nearly uniform epoch instead, the model describes everything since with the same continuous cosmic time, so the picture of an unbroken succession of slices holds over the span the model covers, whatever came before, though that's still a model-based conclusion rather than a proof about the whole spacetime. On the standard model, our universe's cosmological time is gapless in the sense that matters at the scales general relativity describes: its values fill an unbroken interval with no first instant, and every worldline passes through every slice between any two it reaches, exactly once. What is a coordinate choice is the neutrino’s slice. Its “now” comes from synchronizing clocks with light signals in its own momentary rest frame, and it changes with its speed. Both slicings can be drawn, but they aren’t on a par: one depends on who’s doing the slicing, and the other doesn’t. That’s why τ works as a universal time standard. It isn’t a clock that sets anyone’s local rate, since local clocks keep their own proper time, but it’s the common time every one of them sits under. One can call a τ-slice “one shared tick of the cosmic clock,” and that’s exactly right. The shift in simultaneity is Δt′ = γ v Δx / c², and Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, so for motion along that line it comes to about γ(v/c) × 2.5 million years. “Thousands of years” corresponds to a few hundred km/s, roughly Earth’s own speed relative to the cosmic background; 370 km/s gives about 3,000 years. None of this makes the τ-slice an absolute “now” in the block universe, where every slice exists equally and none is special. A clock can still be deliberately set to τ: read the local CMB temperature to find your cosmic time, and use the dipole to correct for your motion. There's a real-world precedent. Devices don't keep a server's time on their own; they sync to it by design, and local clocks relate to τ the same way.
